# Daughter of the North — Chapter 001: Merewic

## The Trade

### Loki

There is a color smoke turns when it has eaten enough thatch to be satisfied — a fat, greasy grey that no longer climbs so much as leans, sagging over the rooftops like a drunk who has found a wall — and Merewic is burning that color now.

I stand at the well in the center of the village with my back to the sea and let it come to me. The smoke. The stink underneath it: fish-oil and scorched wool and the wet-iron of blood cooling on packed earth. Beneath that, older than the fire, the smell the whole coast wears — salt and weed-rot and the tarred hemp of nets left to dry on racks that will not see another season. Mid-morning light comes down through the haze the color of thin mead, and it makes everything look gilded. Even the bodies. Especially the bodies.

Seventy men came off four keels at first light. The *Úlfsvaka* took the shingle first because she always does — I do not send my pack anywhere I have not already put my own boots — and *Járnkjaftr*, *Skarfr*, and *Gjallar* ground in behind her close enough that a man could step from gunwale to gunwale without wetting his feet. Out past the breaker line, where the water goes from green to the black of true cold, the rest of my fleet holds the crescent. Twenty-one prows facing the land like a jaw half-closed. The four knarrs sit fat and patient at the center of the curve, sails furled, hulls empty, waiting to be fed.

The fight itself was nothing. I want to say that plainly, because men will make it larger in the halls this winter and the telling is not the thing. Fishermen with boat-hooks. A smith with his hammer. One grey-headed fool with an old ash spear who knew how to hold it and died anyway with my seax under his jaw and his own blood filling his mouth faster than he could curse me. The Saxons on this stretch have not seen a real raid in a generation — you can read it in how they built. No ditch. No palisade. Huts strung out along the strand like beads off a broken cord. They ran, and some of them are still running somewhere up in the tree line, and I have not bothered to send men after them. The woods can keep what the woods catch.

What I did not expect — what has had my jaw set like a fist since the first door came off its hinges — is what we found *behind* the doors.

---

"Eleven marks in hacksilver from the smith's alone." Jensen's voice is low and dry as a whetstone. "Not the smithy. His *house*. Under the hearthstone. Wrapped in a cloth that cost more than the hearth."

He stands at my left shoulder. He has stood at my left shoulder since we were boys in my father's yards learning that a shield is not a thing you hide behind but a thing you *push* with, and in fourteen years I have never once had to look to know he was there. He is a hand shorter than I am and built leaner — all cord and long bone, the kind of frame that lies about how hard it hits — with dark hair cropped close and a face that gives the world nothing it has not paid for. There is blood drying in the seams of his knuckles. There is a hammer of black iron no longer than my thumb hanging at his throat on a plaited cord, and before he speaks a number he touches it. He has done this since he was six. He does not know he does it. His grandmother put the hammer around his neck and the Old Ways in his marrow before Ott ever put a blade in his hand, and there are men who have laughed at that. Not for long. Not twice.

He holds the tallies. Two.

The first is a stave of split hazel, pale-fleshed, notched down its length with a knife — a notch for each mark of silver, a longer cut for each slave, a cross-cut for each head of livestock. That one is my father's. It will be handed to Ott's steward when the knarrs unload at the fjord, and the steward will run his thumb down it and weigh the silver against it and find that they agree, because Jensen has never in his life recorded a false number. Ask him the count and he tells you the count. It is why I trust him with the second.

The second is a length of sinew cord, knotted in a pattern that only he and I can read, and he keeps it inside his tunic against the skin over his heart where the sweat of his body has darkened it to the color of old leather. That one is the truth.

Ott takes a third off the top of every raid I bring home. That is the Jarl's due, and I do not begrudge it — a man who sits in the Great Seat is owed the weight of it. But a third becomes two-fifths on the steward's scales, and two-fifths becomes half by the time the silver has been "counted for the Jarl's stores," and no one says anything because Ott is Ott and I am his bastard warhound and hounds do not audit their masters. So what reaches my father's hall is what the hazel says. And what reaches the seventy men who put their shoulders to my shield-wall this morning, and what goes into the ground beneath the black oak floor of my own longhouse, is the difference between hazel and sinew.

My father made me a weapon. He gave a great deal of thought to the edge. He did not, I think, ever consider who a weapon belongs to once the hand holding it goes slack. Six sworn captains sail under my wolf-prow and not his because of that cord. They know whose count they are on.

"The Elder's house?"

Jensen touches the hammer. "Two silver-gilt cups. Frankish work, the pattern on the foot is Rhenish. A cloak-pin — gold, garnet inlay, the pin itself is silver-wire. Forty-three coins in a leather bag under the floor. Frisian, mostly. Some Mercian. Four with a script none of us can read." A pause. "Eastern. The kind that comes up the rivers through Gardariki."

"In a fishing village."

"In a fishing village."

I look out over Merewic and try to make the arithmetic sit. Twenty-some huts. A smokehouse. A boat-shed with six hulls in it, decent hulls, cared-for. A little stone church at the north end with a wooden cross that Kaleb has already knocked off its perch for the joy of watching it fall. The kind of place that should give up a few marks of hacksilver, a sack of barley, some salted herring, a few strong backs for the thrall-market at Kaupang — the kind of place I chose because a Frisian trader in my father's hall swore over his third horn that the Saxon coast south of the great river had gone soft and fat and no one was watching it.

He was right about the fat. I do not think he knew *how* right.

A hoard. That is the shape my mind keeps reaching for, because it is the only shape that fits. Some Saxon lordling's plunder buried here a generation back and dug up piece by piece. A church's treasury smuggled out ahead of a fire. A ship gone aground with its belly full and the whole village taking a silent share. Something hidden. Something they have been sitting on the way a hen sits on eggs, warm and stupid and certain that no fox would ever find the coop.

"Six horses," Jensen says.

I glance at him.

"In the long shed past the church. Six. Good ones — two would carry a mailed man. Fishermen don't keep six horses. They can't feed six horses." His dark eyes are on the tree line, not on me. "And the herbs. Every hut has bundles hanging. Not cooking herbs. Yarrow, comfrey, feverfew, something with a purple root I don't know. Enough in the Elder's house alone to dose the whole fleet through a winter."

"Perhaps they're sickly."

"Perhaps." He says it the way he says everything — flat, exact, with the weight of a stone set down on a table. He will not theorize. He has never theorized. He gives me what his eyes have seen and lets me carry it, and I have learned over fourteen years that when Jensen says *perhaps* in that voice, he does not mean perhaps at all. "There's a path from the woods. Northeast, past the horse-shed. Worn to the clay. A hand deep. That's years of feet."

I file it. I do not know what to do with it, so I file it, the way I file most things Jensen hands me — in the place behind my teeth where I keep what I have not yet decided is important.

The truth is I do not much care *how* they came by it. Silver is silver. It weighs the same on the steward's scales whether it came out of a Saxon lord's treasury or a Saxon fisherman's hearth, and it will buy the same iron, the same grain, the same loyalty in the same dark halls. My father will be pleased. Not *pleased* — Ott does not do pleased, Ott does a kind of narrowing of the eyes that means a man has failed to disappoint him — but the hazel stave will be long enough this time that even Halldis, hunched at his elbow with her dead eye rolled toward the rafters, will not find a way to sour it.

I think of her without wanting to. The night before we sailed, in the Great Hall, with the fires banked low and my father drinking and my men loud at the lower tables — she threw the bones. Not for me. She has never once thrown for me; I am a bastard and a killer and she has made it clear for nineteen years that the gods do not stoop to men like me. She threw for Ott. And when the knuckle-bones fell she looked at them a long time, longer than usual, and then she looked at me across the hall with the good eye and the dead one together, and she said to my father in a voice pitched to carry: *The Wolf brings back salt in an open wound.*

Ott laughed. I spat on the floor between the benches. And I have not thought of it since, and I am thinking of it now for no reason at all, and I do not like that I am.

Ylva asked for blue glass beads. Frankish, the good kind, the ones that catch the fire. And a Saxon girl for the washing, because the last one died of the cough in the spring and Ylva's hands are cracking. I look toward the pen and think: there will be girls enough. I will pick one later. Or I will let Kaleb pick, and he will pick the one with the best tits, and Ylva will thank him for it and then work the girl half to death, because Ylva has never once in nine years let another woman in my house forget whose house it is.

---

The pen is a hurdle fence — woven hazel panels that the villagers used for their sheep, hauled together by my men and lashed at the corners with the villagers' own rope. Forty-odd Saxons inside it, maybe fifty. The ones who did not run and did not fight. Women, mostly. Children. A few old men with the grey, sunken look of people who have already decided how this ends and are only waiting for the ending to arrive. Four of my men stand guard with spears grounded and shields slung, and they are bored, and bored men with spears are dangerous to whatever is in front of them, so I have put Hauk in charge of that fence. Hauk does not get bored. Hauk gets *hungry*, which is a different problem, but a predictable one.

The rest of the pack is doing what men do when the killing is finished and the sun is up and the blood in them has not yet gone cold.

Ásgeir and two of *Gjallar*'s men are inside the church, and I can hear them from here — the crack of an axe into a chest that will not open, the laughter when it finally does. Torvald has a cauldron over his shoulder and a bolt of dyed wool under the other arm and is walking toward the shingle with the stately, satisfied gait of a man carrying his winter. Behind the smokehouse, out of the direct line of the pen, a woman is crying in the particular exhausted rhythm that means she has been at it a while and no longer expects anyone to come. Someone has a girl bent over a fish-gutting table by the boat-shed and there are three men waiting, passing a skin of the village's own ale between them, not watching, just waiting the way men wait for a ferry.

I look at all of it and it is weather to me. It is what happens when you build on a coast without a ditch. It is what happened to my mother's village before I was born and it is why I am standing here with green eyes and a Saxon length of bone in a Norse skin, and I have never once felt the need to explain that to anyone, least of all myself.

Then Kaleb roars.

---

"*HOLD!*"

It is not a word so much as a thing thrown — a great flat slab of sound that comes from the bottom of his chest and hits the whole village at once, and I have heard him use that voice on a shield-wall that was breaking and watched it un-break. Every man in Merewic stops where he stands. Ásgeir's axe comes down half-swung. Torvald turns with the cauldron still on his shoulder. The three men at the boat-shed look up, and the one at the table stops mid-thrust with his breeches at his knees and his face gone stupid with interruption.

Kaleb comes down the middle of the village like he owns it, which for the length of this morning is very nearly true.

He is grinning. He is always grinning; I have seen him grin with a Frankish arrow through the meat of his thigh and a Saxon spear-shaft in both hands that he'd just wrenched out of the Saxon. He is my height less a finger, broad through the chest, a long-legged man with a face the gods built for charm and a body they built for slaughter — hair the brown of wet bark tied back at the nape, a beard he keeps short so it does not catch in the chinstrap, eyes the grey of a winter sea. There is a slash on his forearm that has bled down over his wrist and soaked the leather of his axe-grip black. There is someone else's blood across his cheek and in the hollow of his throat, sprayed in the fine pattern that means an artery, and he has not wiped it off because Kaleb has never in his life wiped off blood before he was finished with the morning.

He is dragging a man by the collar.

Not dragging — *walking* him, one big hand knotted in the fine wool at the back of the man's neck the way you carry a dog that has shat on the rushes, and the man's feet are moving but they are not doing the work. The man is perhaps fifty. Silver at the temples, a face made for judging — long, clean-shaven in the Saxon way, with deep grooves beside the mouth that speak of a lifetime spent frowning at other men's business. And he is dressed like no fisherman I have ever pulled out of a hut.

The tunic is blue. Not the greyed, washed-out blue you get from a peasant's woad-vat, but a deep, saturated color that took many dippings and a dye-master who knew his trade, with a border of silver thread worked in a running pattern at hem and cuff. Over it a cloak of black wool so fine it moves like water, pinned at the shoulder with a brooch the size of a child's palm — gold, with four garnets set in the arms of a cross and a fifth, larger, at the center where they meet. There are rings on his fingers. Three on the left hand alone. His shoes are leather, soft, dyed to match the tunic, and they are the only things about him that look like they have never been wet.

Kaleb brings him to a stop three paces from me and holds him there, and the whole village holds with him. I feel seventy pairs of eyes turn. I feel the Saxons in the pen turn too, the way a field of grass turns when the wind changes.

"Brother," Kaleb says. He is breathing a little hard and the grin has not moved a hair's width. "I found the fattest one. Took me a while. He was under a bed, which — I want to say this for the men, so they know the standard — is a place I *did* look, and I want it remembered that I looked there *first*, and it was the *second* bed." He gives the man a small shake, the way you shake a sack to settle the grain. "He's got a whole hall's worth of gold on his back and he's very keen to talk. Mostly in Saxon, which is a shame, because I only know the words for *fuck* and *mercy* and I've heard both a great deal this morning."

I do not answer Kaleb. I look at the Saxon.

He is not looking at me. He is looking at the pen.

I follow his eyes because a man's eyes go where his heart is, and I want to know where this one's heart is before I decide what to cut. They go to the near corner of the hurdle-fence, where a girl kneels with the other women — sixteen, perhaps seventeen, with a fall of yellow hair the color of ripe barley that some man has already had his hands in, judging by the state of the braid, and a face that will be beautiful for another ten years and is beautiful now in the hard, unformed way of a thing that has never once been told no. Her dress is green wool. Good green. Her hands are folded in her lap and they are white-knuckled and they are wearing rings.

Daughter. The bones of her face say it. The way the man's whole body has leaned toward her without his feet moving says it louder.

"Lord." The Saxon finds his voice, and it is a voice used to being listened to — a deep, rounded speaker's voice, trained on a lifetime of standing up in a hall and telling other men what was fair. It is speaking Norse, badly. The Norse of a man who has traded with Danes at the river-mouth and picked up enough to argue a price. "Lord. Great — great lord. I am Edrik. I am — the *Elder*, I am the head of this — I speak for them. All of them." A gesture toward the pen, rings flashing. "Spare them. Spare their lives, and I will — I have — there is a *trade*. Lord. Please."

Then he does what frightened men always do, which is slide into the tongue he was born in because he thinks it will carry his meaning better.

"*Ic bidde þē*—" *I beg you.* "*Nim hit. Nim hit eall. Ac lǣt hīe libban.*" *Take it. Take it all. But let them live.*

I keep my face exactly where it is.

He does not know that I understand him. None of them know. It is not a thing I advertise — that the Wolf of the Fjord suckled on Saxon and heard his first songs in it and still, on the worst nights, dreams in the tongue of a dead thrall-woman who used to sing him to sleep in a corner of a kennel. My men know I speak *some*. They do not know how much. And these people — this fat, stupid, rich village — have been talking freely around me all morning, in the pen and on the strand and dragged out of their doors by the hair, and I have heard every word of it and given them nothing back but green eyes and iron.

So I hear the trader's-Norse and I hear the Saxon under it, and I hear the thing he is not saying in either, which is that the *them* he is begging for has a very specific shape and that shape is wearing green wool and kneeling in the corner of a sheep-pen with her father's rings on her fingers.

"A trade," I say. My own Norse is slow, deliberate, the register I use when I want a man to understand that every word has been weighed before it left my mouth. "You have something I have not already taken."

"Yes. Yes, lord."

"You have this whole village's silver under your floor and a king's brooch on your shoulder and there is something *else*." I let it sit. "Something better."

His throat works. "Better than — yes. Lord. Better than silver."

And there it is. The shape my mind has been reaching for since the first door came off its hinges. A hoard. A hidden thing, a buried thing, the wellspring that all this Frankish gilt and Frisian coin has been trickling out of for a generation. He has it. He has been sitting on it. And now he is going to hand it to me to buy the life of a yellow-haired girl in a green dress, and I am going to take it, and then I am going to decide what his daughter is worth on my own scales, because a man who bargains from his knees does not get to set the terms.

I look at Kaleb. He raises his eyebrows a fraction, which is Kaleb for *your call, and I already know what it is.*

"You have my ear," I tell Edrik. "You do not have my word. Show me what you're selling. Then we'll talk about what it buys."

He nods. He nods too many times, the way a man does when he is not really hearing anymore but only agreeing, and then he turns his head toward the tree line and shouts — in Saxon, high and cracked — "*Wulfstan! Ceolred! Bringaþ hīe!*"

*Bring her.*

I hear it. I file it. I do not let it reach my face.

---

The path Jensen spoke of comes down out of the woods between the horse-shed and the church — a groove in the earth worn deep enough that the grass on either side stands above it like the banks of a stream — and two men come down it.

Big men. Fishermen's shoulders, fishermen's hands. The larger of the two has a face like a fist and a beard gone grey at the chin, and the other is younger, rawer, with a fresh cut over one eye that he must have taken this morning and forgotten about. They are afraid — the whole village is afraid, it is the natural state of a village with my ships on its beach — but their fear has a strange shape to it. It is not pointed at me.

It is pointed at what they are carrying between them.

You.

I do not know you are *you* yet. I will not know that for a long time, and when I finally understand it I will not be able to say when the knowing began. But I mark the moment anyway, the way a man marks the moment a blade goes into him — not by the pain, which comes later, but by the strange, sudden *stillness* of the flesh around the wound.

You are fighting them. Not the way the women in the pen fought, with their hands up and their voices high — you are fighting the way a caught animal fights, with your whole body, silent except for the breath tearing in and out of you and a low, continuous sound in the back of your throat that is not quite a growl and not quite anything else. Your wrists are bound in front of you with rough hemp cord, wound three times and knotted hard, and the skin under the rope is raw. Your feet are bare. They are cut — I can see the dark smears on the clay where you have been dragged — and you are digging them into the earth anyway, heels first, so that the two men have to half-carry you and their own feet slip in the churned mud of the path.

You are filthy. Mud to the thigh. Something dark — blood, old blood, not yours by the look of it — dried in a long smear down the front of a dress that was grey wool once and is now the color of a riverbed. Your hair hangs over your face in thick, matted ropes, so caked with dirt and damp that it takes me two breaths to understand what I am looking at underneath it, and when I understand it, the thought lands in me with an odd, flat weight, like a stone dropped in a well that is deeper than you thought:

*White.*

Not yellow. Not the barley-gold of the girl in the green dress. *White* — the white of new frost on a black rock, the white of the fjord in the hour before the sun clears the mountains, that impossible no-color that only exists in the far north on the coldest mornings of the year. It is so pale that even under the filth it seems to gather what little light this smoke-thick morning has to give and hold it, and I have never in twenty-eight years seen hair that color on a living head.

They throw you down at my feet.

Not set. *Throw* — a two-handed heave, the way you throw a net or a sack of wet fish, and you hit the mud on your knees and your bound hands and the impact drives a sound out of you that is half breath and half fury. Mud sprays my boots. For an instant you are still, bent over your own bound wrists with your white hair hanging in the muck, and I see the backs of your hands.

They are marked.

*Runes.* Black as charcoal, sharp as knife-cuts, running over your knuckles and across the backs of your hands and up under the hemp cord into the raw skin of your wrists. Not scratched, not inked in the crude way of sailors and thralls — *set*, into the skin, with a clarity and depth that my eye keeps refusing to accept as anything a needle could do. I know runes. Every man on this beach knows runes; we carve them on our blades and our prows and our dead. I know Halldis has three on the inside of her left forearm that she says a völva in Uppsala gave her when she was a girl, and they look like a child's scratching next to what is on your hands.

Two of these I recognize. One I half-recognize. The rest I have never seen in my life.

And then you move.

You come up off your knees so fast that both village men flinch back — not toward them, not toward me, but *sideways*, toward the gap between the horse-shed and the burning smokehouse, and you are quick, gods, you are *quick*, bound and barefoot and bleeding and you have covered half the distance before the larger man's brain catches up to his eyes.

He catches you. Not cleanly — a hand in your hair, a wrench that snaps your head back and turns you — and then he hits you.

Open-handed. Full arm. The crack of it goes through the silence of the village like an axe into wet wood, and you go down again, hard, on your side in the mud with your bound hands thrown up across your face, and there is blood at the corner of your mouth now, bright against the dirt. The man stands over you breathing through his nose with his hand still raised and I see his face and there is something on it that is not fear at all.

It is *satisfaction*. Old, worn-in satisfaction, the look of a man who has wanted to do that for a very long time and has finally found an hour in which no one will stop him.

"*Wicce*," he says, not loud, almost to himself. And spits into the mud beside your head.

*Witch.*

My men laugh. Of course they laugh — a bound woman going down under a Saxon's hand is worth a laugh on any beach in the world. Kaleb laughs, a short bark of it, and I hear Hauk from the pen saying something about the Saxons having *finally* found a use for their hands.

I am not laughing.

Something in me has gone very still. Not the stillness of shock — I have not been shocked by anything since I was twelve years old and came home from the strand with a boy's tooth stuck in the knuckle of my right hand — but the other kind. The kind that comes over me in the last breath before a shield-wall meets, when the noise falls away and the world narrows to the width of a blade and everything in me that is not the killing goes quiet and waits its turn.

Edrik is speaking. I hear it from a long way off. "Yours, lord. She is yours. Take her — she is *yours*, take her and go and spare us, that is the trade, that is what I—"

I do not look at Edrik.

I look at the hand. The Saxon's right hand, the one he struck you with, still half-raised, the fingers red where they met your face. Wulfstan — that was the name, the Elder shouted it up the path — Wulfstan the fisherman, with his grey chin and his fist of a face and his hand red from a woman's skin.

A thing was being handed to me. Whatever this is — whoever you are, whatever *wicce* means to these people that it should make two grown men more afraid of a bound girl than of seventy Norsemen — you were being placed in front of the Wolf of the Fjord as payment, in front of his men, in the sight of his ships. You were, from the moment Edrik opened his mouth, already the Wolf's to accept or refuse.

And this Saxon fisherman put his hand on you.

I do not decide to move. I notice I have moved. The seax on my left hip is out — the shorter one, the nine-inch one with the pattern-welded blade and the antler grip worn dark by fourteen years of my palm — and I have crossed the three paces between us and I have driven it in under the point of Wulfstan's jaw at the angle that finds the great vessel and opens it, and I have turned my wrist, and I have stepped back.

It is very fast. It is the fastest thing I do. Kaleb says I kill like a man swatting a fly, which is not quite right — a man swatting a fly is angry, and I am not angry. I am simply *correcting* something.

Wulfstan's hand comes up to his throat and finds that his throat is no longer there in the way he remembers it. His eyes go wide and very white. He makes a sound like a bellows with a hole in it. Then his knees unlock and he goes down into the mud beside you, on his face, and the blood comes out of him in the great, pumping, arm-thick surges of a man who has been opened properly, and it runs into the churned clay and it runs toward your bare feet, and the younger one — Ceolred — has gone the color of new cheese and is backing away with both hands up and his mouth working on a prayer.

No one is laughing now.

The silence in Merewic is total. I can hear the fire in the thatch. I can hear the sea. I can hear Edrik's breath going in and out of him like something trapped.

I wipe the seax on Wulfstan's tunic — his good tunic, his Sunday wool, dyed the same watered blue as the Elder's — and I sheathe it, and then I turn my head and look at Edrik.

"That," I say, in my slow Norse, "was for the hand."

He is shaking. All of him. The garnets on his brooch tremble with it.

"You offered me a thing. Your man struck it before I'd said whether I'd have it." I let my eyes rest on him, pale and flat and patient. "In front of my men. Do you know what my men would think of me if I let a Saxon fisherman put his hand on what's being sold to the Wolf? They would think I was a man who could be *handled*." A pause. "I am not that man, Edrik. Do not make me show you again."

"N-no. No, lord. Lord, I—"

"We have not discussed the rest."

I leave him there with that. I leave him with the shape of it — that the trade is not made, that the yellow-haired girl in the green dress is still kneeling in a sheep-pen with my men's eyes on her, that he has spent his one card and I have not yet told him what it bought.

And I crouch.

---

You are lying on your side in the mud with Wulfstan's blood pooling warm against your ankles and your bound hands still up in front of your face, and you have not moved since he hit you. Not frozen — I know what frozen looks like, and this is not it. This is *waiting*. Every line of you is coiled. The breath is going in and out of you fast and shallow through your nose and I can see the pulse hammering in the side of your throat, under the mud, under —

Under more marks.

They climb your throat. Black lines rising from the collar of the ruined dress, up over the tendon and the hollow beneath your jaw, fine as spider-silk and dense as a forest, curling around the column of your neck in patterns that make my eye slide off them the way it slides off a thing it has been told not to look at. They are not the same as the ones on your hands. The ones on your hands are *clean* — hard-edged, purposeful, the look of a thing made for use. These are something else. Older-looking. Stranger. Like the ones on your hands are words and these are the sound of someone breathing behind a door.

I put two fingers under your jaw and turn your face up out of the mud.

Your skin is cold. Your jaw is small in my hand — I could close my fingers around it entirely — and the bones under it are fine and hard as a bird's. The mud is smeared across one cheek and there is blood on your lip, a bright bead of it that has already started to run, and I see for the first time that under the filth your skin is *white*, not the fair of my own Saxon-bred hide but the true bloodless porcelain of a thing kept out of the sun, and that your mouth is full and torn and set in a line that is not fear.

And then you open your eyes and look at me.

*Amber.* That is the first word. You pay it, and then you *come back*, litla hrafn, because I have not finished with you and I do not give up things I have not finished with."

You do not answer.

I watch you not answer. I watch the words reach you and I watch you weigh them and I watch you *choose* — your mouth opens, a breath, and closes — and the amber eyes hold mine and they are wet and they are steady and they are not lying, because you will not lie to me, I understand that in the silence more clearly than I have understood anything on this beach. You do not know if you are coming back. You will not say that you are. And you will not say that you are not, either, because a part of you — the part that bucked against my cock, the part that made that sound — does not want the last word you say to me to be *no*.

So you say nothing. And you step back out of my hands, and you turn, and you walk to the stone.

---

I take my place at the edge of the ring.

One boot-length outside the circle of black stones. Jensen said no man inside, and Jensen has not been wrong about a holy thing in twenty-six years of being alive, and I am not fool enough to test him with you standing on the other side of the line. The men fall back behind me to ten paces at a word from Kaleb that I do not hear him give — I feel it, the fan widening, the spears coming up off the ground and then grounding again further back, the particular hush of seventy men settling into the posture of witnesses. Jensen is at my left shoulder. Kaleb at my right. The three of us at the edge of the ring like a shield-wall three men wide, and behind us the pack, and behind them the Saxons on their knees, and out on the water two of them bound to a post with the tide at their chests, and above all of it the smoke-stained sky of a late morning in the dying of the year, blue through the thinning haze, with the sun a white coin in the south.

You stand at the land-face of the altar with your back to me. Three paces. I could cross them in one.

The wolf has placed itself at your right hip. The raven has lifted from your shoulder — a crack of wings, a short beat upward — and settled on the top-slab of the stone itself, on the sea-side edge, where it folds and sits like a carving and turns its head to look at me over the offerings with one black eye.

And you lift your face to the sky, and you speak.

"Hear me, Fjörgyn. Ægir."

The Norse comes out of you in a voice I have not heard you use. Not the harbor-wine alto. Not the softness for the raven or the boy. This is *carrying*, pitched from the bottom of the chest to reach the edge of the water and the top of the tree line and whatever is listening past both, and it does not sound like a woman's voice and it does not sound like a man's. It sounds like a horn. It sounds like the hall-call. And it is *clean*, every vowel of it shaped the way the old men shape them, the way Jensen's grandmother shaped them, the way no one under forty in my father's fjord can be bothered to shape them anymore.

"You know me. Lend me your attention once more, on this land I have asked so much from you for."

You bring the seax up.

I see it come up in your right hand and I see your left hand open beside it, palm up, written, and I see what you are going to do a half-heartbeat before you do it and I do not move, because I said I would not, and because the thing I am about to watch is a thing I have done to my own forearm a hundred times before a hundred stones and have never once seen done the way you do it.

You draw the blade across your palm.

Not a nick. Not the shallow sting a man gives himself for show at a Jól-stone. You lay the edge of my seax into the heel of your left hand below the fingers and you *draw*, the whole nine inches of pattern-welded steel sliding through the written skin from the root of the little finger to the root of the thumb in one long smooth unhurried pull, and the flesh opens behind it like a mouth. Deep. I see the pale shine of the fat under the skin before the blood comes, and then the blood comes, welling up thick and dark and fast, filling the cut, filling the cup of your palm, running over the heel of your hand and down the inside of your wrist. And you do not flinch. Not a hitch. Not a sound. Your face is turned up to the sky and it does not move, and you shift the seax to your left hand — the cut hand, the grip going slick and red — and you lay the blade into your right palm and you do it again.

Behind me a man makes a sound in his throat. Not Kaleb. Not Jensen. Further back. One of the young ones.

You drop the seax.

It falls point-down into the clay at the altar's foot and stands there quivering, and the sound it makes going in is the only sound on the beach, and you hold out your hands. Both of them. Palms up, over the bowl of the stone, over the barley and the boar and the braid of your own hair, and the blood runs from the cuts in two thick ropes and falls onto the night-stone and spreads across the dished center and finds the channel.

The channel takes it. I watch it go. I watch your blood run the length of that dark groove toward the near edge the way a hundred autumns' worth of it has run before, and reach the lip, and fall from the lip to the earth at the altar's foot in a thin steady stream that darkens the clay and pools there and *soaks in* — faster than it should, faster than clay drinks, as though the ground under that stone were thirsty in a way the rest of the beach was not.

The wolf tips its head back and howls.

I have heard wolves. I have lain awake in the hungry-months with my father's hunting-dogs whining and shivering under the bench while the packs came down out of the fells and sang at the walls of the Hall, and I know the sound — the long rising wail, the break at the top, the fall — and this is that sound the way the raven's cry was a raven's cry. It is *bigger*. It comes out of a chest the size of a man's and it goes up through the registers a wolf should have and keeps going, up past the break, past the place where a wolf's voice would crack into a yelp, into a long held note so high and so clear and so *full* that I feel it in the plates of my skull, and it does not fall. It holds. It holds and holds while the blood runs from your palms onto the stone and the hair stands up on my arms and the sixty-two Saxons behind me begin, as one, to weep.

And the raven answers.

It comes up off the top-slab in a single explosion of black wings and climbs — straight up, not circling, straight up into the thinning smoke like a thrown spear — and when it reaches the height of the tallest thatch it opens its iron bill and lets out the cry it gave over the sea, the horn-cry, the *stop-what-you-are-doing* cry, and this time the cry has a shape to it that the first one did not. It is pointed. It is aimed at the sky like a spear-thrust. And the nearest villagers go over backward in the mud with their arms over their heads and three of my men behind me take a step back without meaning to, and I hear the grounding-thud of their spear-butts and I hear Hauk snarl at them to hold, and I hold.

The sky turns.

I have sailed twenty-eight years and I have seen weather come. I have seen the black squall-line come down off the fells at midsummer so fast that a man had time to reef and not to pray. I have seen the great winter gales build for three days out of a sky gone the color of a bruise. I have never seen *this*. One breath the sky above Merewic is a late-autumn blue going hazy at the edges with the smoke, the sun a white coin in the south, and the next breath there are clouds, and the clouds are not *coming* from anywhere. They are not rolling in off the sea. They are not building up over the land. They are simply *there*, where they were not, black and dense and piled and swollen with a weight that presses down on the top of my head like a hand, and they are spreading outward from a point directly above the stone the way ink spreads in water. West to the horizon. East to the tree line. South over the sun, which goes from white to yellow to a dull copper smear and then is gone entirely, and the light on the beach drops in the space of three heartbeats from morning to the thick purple-grey of a winter dusk. And the air *changes*. It goes heavy and wet and cold and it *crawls*. I feel it on the backs of my hands and the back of my neck, the skin lifting, the hairs rising one by one, that electric, breathless, tooth-aching pressure that comes in the last moment before a lightning-strike close enough to deafen — and somewhere out past the breaker line where my fleet rides the crescent, thunder speaks. Once. Long and low and rolling, from one end of the sky to the other, and the shingle under my boots hums with it.

My ships. The thought comes and goes like a gull. Twenty-one keels and four knarrs sitting at anchor under *that*, with Bersi of the *Járnkjaftr* and Thorolf of the *Skarfr* and the sworn captains looking up from their decks at a storm that was not there a hundred heartbeats ago, and no way to reach them, and no way to tell them to hold, and nothing to do but trust that men who have followed my wolf-prow for ten years will not cut their cables for weather. I put it down. I cannot hold it and watch you at once, and I cannot look away from you.

Because the stone is burning.

There is no torch. I want that set down in whatever account of this is made, by whatever skald my father's hall produces to make it smaller than it was. There was no torch and there was no oil and there was no flint struck and no coal carried and no man's hand near that stone but hers. One moment your blood is running down the channel into thirsty clay, and the next moment — between one roll of the thunder and the next — the surface of the night-stone is *alight*. All of it. The whole top-slab, bowl and channel and offerings and the raven's empty perch, gone up in a single silent sheet of flame that stands a hand's height above the black rock and does not flicker, does not dance, does not do any of the things fire does, but simply *stands*, a flat still plane of light with the sheaf of barley and the three apples and the carved boar and the wooden prick all standing inside it untouched, as though the fire were made of glass and they had been set beneath it.

And it is *blue*.

Not the blue at the root of a hearth-flame where it meets the wood. Not the blue of a sulphur-match. The blue of the heart of the wolf's eyes. The blue of deep ice. A blue so pale at the center that it is white, so white that it hurts, a white-blue that casts no shadow and throws no warmth — I feel it, I am three paces from the stone and I feel *nothing* on my face, no heat at all — and the air above it does not shimmer the way air shimmers over fire but goes *clear*, hard-edged, as though the fire were burning the haze out of it, and from the edges of the stone where the blue light meets the black rock a thin white rime begins to spread. Frost. On a burning altar. Crawling outward from the flame across the dressed faces of the night-stone like breath across a cold blade, feathering, branching, glittering in the blue light, and the breath that comes out of my mouth when I finally let it fogs in the air in front of me like a winter morning.

The runes are drinking.

I see it first on your forearms, because your arms are still out over the stone with the blood running from your palms into the fire — into it, the blood falls through the blue flame and does not sizzle, does not smoke, simply *goes*, as though the fire were taking it directly — and the marks on your forearms, the ones that run up from the cut wrists to the elbow, are *lit*. Not reflecting the blue. *Holding* it. Each line of them gone from black to a glowing pewter to the same ice-white as the flame, so bright that I can see them through the back of your arms, so bright that the shape of the bones shows dark inside the light, and the light is *moving*. Up. From your palms, up the wrists, up the forearms, under the torn sleeves, and I see it come out at the collar — the marks on your throat, the ones I had my tongue on, catching, one after another, a chain of white fire climbing the column of your neck toward the hinge of your jaw. And at your back. Through the ruined wool of the dress, down the length of your spine from the nape to the small of the back, a line of light so fierce that the cloth goes *translucent* with it, and I see the shape of the marks beneath — long and twisting and dense, nothing like the clean cuts on your hands, a knotted ladder of fire running the whole length of your backbone — and those marks are not pewter and they are not white.

They are *dark*. They are lit from within like the others and they are *dark* at the same time, a burning blue-black like the heart of a bruise, like the color at the bottom of a sea-trench where the light has given up, and they drink the blue fire off the stone the way a drunkard drinks, greedy and fast and without any evidence of ever being satisfied, and the hair on my arms, which has not lain down since the wolf came out of the smoke, tries to stand up further and cannot.

*Shadow.* The word comes into my mind and I do not know where from. *Skuggi.* The raven's name. The marks on your spine are the color the raven is.

The fire climbs.

The standing sheet of blue flame on the altar's top begins to *rise*. Not spreading — rising, drawing itself up from the four edges of the stone toward the center, gathering, the way water gathers in a bowl when the bowl is tilted, and from the center it goes *up*. A column. A pillar of cold light the width of the bowl, climbing into the black sky with the slow inevitable speed of a mast being stepped, ten feet, twenty, forty, past the height of the tallest thatch and past the height of the church-cross Kaleb knocked down and still going, a shaft of white-blue fire standing on a black stone and reaching up into a storm, and where its top meets the belly of the clouds the clouds *light* from within, a vast slow pulse of blue through the black like a heart beating in a body made of night. The whole beach is lit by it. Every face. Every spearhead. The sea gone silver and the shingle gone blue and the two shapes at the post out in the water with the tide at their throats turned to carved things, and the shadows — there are no shadows. Nothing on this beach is casting a shadow. A pillar of fire the height of a mast stands twenty paces from me and *nothing has a shadow*.

And your eyes.

You turn your head. Not toward me. Toward the pillar, your face lifted into its light, and I see your profile against the blue, and I see your eye, and it is *white*. Not rolled back. Not the whites showing in a fit. The whole of the eye — the amber, the dark ring, the pupil — gone to a flat lightless milk-white like the eye of a boiled fish, like the dead eye in Halldis's face, and it is not *seeing* this beach. I know it is not. I know it the way I would know a man's eye had gone across the shield-wall to somewhere I could not follow. You are looking at something a long way past the pillar and the storm, and your mouth is moving.

Chanting. I hear it under the thunder and the howl and the roar of the pillar, which does roar, a low vast rushing like wind through a sea-cave. Norse. *Galdr*, the long wavering spell-chant, but not the way Halldis does it — not the whine, not the trance-mutter — *clean*, each word shaped and placed and sent up the pillar like a ring threaded on a line, and I catch some of them. *Hlið*. Gate. *Opna*. Open. *Heyr mik*. Hear me. *Ek kalla*. I call. Over and over, the same shapes in different orders, the voice climbing with the fire, and the wolf's howl has not stopped and the raven is a black mote circling the pillar's crown where it meets the burning clouds and Jensen beside me is *speaking*.

Not loud. Under his breath. His lips moving. And I turn my head and I look at my brother and I see that his face is wet, that there are tears running down into the stubble of his jaw and he has not noticed them, that his hand on the hammer has gone so white that the iron has cut into his palm and there is blood on his wrist, and I see what he is saying.

The same words. He is saying your words. Half a heartbeat behind you, in the same clean old shapes, *hlið, opna, heyr mik*, his grandmother's chant, the one he has not spoken since she died, coming out of him on a Saxon beach as though it had been waiting twenty years behind his teeth for someone to say it first.

"Jensen —"

"It is a *calling*." He does not look at me. His voice is cracked and hoarse and he does not stop chanting between his words to me; he threads them through. "Not a blessing. Not an offering. She has made the *vé* and bled on it and lit it and now she is — *opna* — she is *asking for a door*, Loki. She is asking the earth and the sea to open the way to the Hall, so that she can give the stone back and — *heyr mik* — take back what she put in it, and that is — that is a thing my grandmother said was done *once*, once, by a völva at Uppsala in the time of the Ynglings, and the völva *died*, Loki, she died with the door open and the Hall looked out through her and —"

"And what."

He does look at me then. Just once. His dark eyes in his wet white face, in the shadowless blue light, and there is nothing in them that I have ever seen there before.

"And it was not the earth that answered."

The pillar *stops*.

I do not mean it goes out. I mean it stops *moving*. The roar cuts off between one heartbeat and the next as though a door had closed on it, and the column of fire stands in the sudden silence perfectly still, not a flicker, not a ripple, a bar of frozen light between the black stone and the black sky, and the wolf's howl stops with it, and the raven's wings, and the thunder, and the wind. Everything stops. The sea stops. I hear it stop — the hiss of the shingle cut off mid-draw, the water standing against the sand like a held breath — and the Saxons stop weeping and my men stop breathing and I stop, I feel myself stop, every nerve in my body gone taut and silent and *waiting*, the way the body waits in the heartbeat before the shield-walls meet, and in that silence, in that total shadowless soundless pressure, I feel a thing I have never felt and will never be able to describe to any man who was not on that beach.

I feel the sky *turn its face*.

That is the only way I have. That is the only shape the thing will take in my mind no matter how many nights I lie awake and try to find a better one. There is a weight above the beach and the weight is a *regard*. Something has been looking somewhere else — at everything, at the whole of the nine worlds and every sparrow falling in them — and has heard its name said in a voice it knows, and has *turned*, and is looking *here*. At this beach. At this stone. At the sixty-two Saxons on their knees and the seventy Northmen at their backs and the drowning man at the post and the wolf and the raven and the white-haired girl with the burning spine, and at me. I feel it find me. I feel it pass over me the way a hand passes over a hide to find the grain, and the pressure of it goes down through the top of my skull and the length of my body and out through the soles of my boots into the earth, and I understand — all at once, totally, the way you understand that you are falling — that I have been *seen*. Not looked at. *Seen*. Every raid and every killing and every woman and every lie I have told my father and every truth I have told no one, seen, in a single instant, by an attention so vast and so old and so entirely without mercy that there is not even room in it for contempt.

My knees want to bend. I feel them want it. I lock them. I do not know what it costs me and I do not care.

Then the voice.

It does not come from the pillar. It does not come from the sky. It does not come from anywhere; it is simply *present*, in the air, in the ground, in the bones of my skull and the hollows of my chest, the way the roll of distant thunder is present — not arriving from a direction but *occurring*, everywhere at once, so low that the first word is felt before it is heard, so vast that it does not seem to come from a throat so much as from the fact of there being a world. And I know the words. That is the thing that undoes me. That is the thing that takes the strength out of my locked knees and nearly puts me in the clay. I *know the words*. Every boy in my father's fjord knows the words; they are the first lay a child learns at the fire, before he can hold a wooden sword, the old man's old lines about the old man's old pain, chanted by grandmothers in cracked voices until the sense wears out of them and only the sound is left. I have heard them a thousand times. I have never once *listened*.

*Veit ek, at ek hekk vindga meiði á* —

*"Nine nights I hung on the windy tree,"* it says, in a voice like the roots of the mountains speaking, *"wounded by spear, given to Óðinn — myself to myself."*

The Saxons scream.

Not all of them. The ones who understand Norse. I hear it go through them like a scythe — the smith's widow, Osric with the hare-lip, three or four others who have traded with Danes at the river-mouth and know enough to catch the shape of what is being said in a voice that is not a man's — and the ones who do not understand scream a heartbeat later because the ones beside them did, and somewhere in the column a woman's voice goes up high and cracked in Saxon, *it is the Devil, it is the Devil speaking*, and no one answers her, because no one on this beach, Saxon or Norse, believes that anymore.

*"Of that tree no man knows from what roots it rises."* The pillar does not move. The frost on the stone has crept to the ground and is spreading outward across the clay in a slow white ring toward the circle of black stones. *"None gave me bread, none gave me drink."*

Jensen goes down.

Not a collapse. One knee. Slowly, the way a man kneels to a king, his right knee finding the clay and his left hand flat on the ground beside it and his right hand still closed on the hammer at his throat, and his head bowed, and his lips still moving — but not the chant now. He is saying the lines. Half a breath behind the voice, in a whisper, the way a child says the words of a lay along with the one who is singing it, *né við hornigi, nýsta ek niðr*, and there are tears running off the end of his nose into the frost and he does not wipe them and he does not stop.

*"I looked down."* The weight of the regard shifts. I feel it move off me and go to the stone, to the pillar, to the white-eyed girl standing in the light with her bleeding hands held out, and the relief of its leaving is so total that I nearly fall. *"I took up the runes — screaming I took them — and fell back from there."*

Silence.

I do not know how long. The sea does not move. The clouds do not move. The frost has reached the ring of black stones and stopped there, a white circle on the clay with the burning altar at its heart, and inside the circle you stand with your face turned up and your eyes gone white and your runes gone white and your spine gone black-burning under the ruined wool, and I understand, in the silence, what I have just been told. Not the words. The *meaning*. I have heard those lines all my life and I have never once understood that they were not a story about power. They are a story about *price*. He did not get the runes. He did not find them. He did not have them given. He hung himself. He put a spear in his own side. He starved and thirsted nine nights in the wind, with no bread and no drink and no man to cut him down, and when the runes came he took them up *screaming* — not in triumph, in *agony* — and fell, and what he is came out of the far side of that. The Allfather is not a thing that was born great. The Allfather is a thing that was *broken*, on purpose, by itself, and then *given*, and then *rose*. And every power he has he *paid for*, eye and breath and blood, and he is telling this beach — he is telling *you* — that the price has not changed.

And you answer him.

I hear your voice go up the pillar in the old tongue and I hear what it says and I will carry what it says to my grave and past it, into whatever hall takes me. You offer him the dead. Wulfstan, and the grey-headed fool with the spear, and the smith, every Saxon who died on this beach this morning, to the sea, to Rán's net, to be carried where the drowned go. You offer him your pain — you name it, piece by piece, in a voice gone hoarse and steady and terrible, *the lip, the cheek, the ribs* (the ribs, I did not know about the ribs, Wulfstan must have kicked you in the hut, I will think of that later and it will make me want to kill him again), *the palms*, and the blood that is still falling from them into the fire. And then you offer him the other things. The things that are not on the body.

*Never having a family.* You say it flat. *Never having a home. Never a kind touch. Never knowing love.* A breath. *Never a man's touch.*

And something in me *revolts*.

It comes up from my gut like sickness. I do not know what it is. I know that you have just offered to a god, as a *sacrifice*, as a thing of *value*, the fact that no man has ever put his hands on you with care, and that you offered it in the same breath as your broken ribs and your bleeding palms, as though it were a wound of the same kind, as though it *hurt the same*, and I understand that it does, and I understand that I had my teeth in your throat and my cock against your belly a hundred heartbeats ago and you kissed me anyway and said *thank you*, and that in twenty-one years that was the closest thing to a kind touch you have ever —

I shut it. I shut it hard, like a door in a gale. I cannot hold that and stand, and I must stand.

*"All that I am,"* you say, to the sky. *"All that I was. All that I may yet be."* Your bleeding hands open wider over the fire. *"Myself to myself."*

The regard comes back.

I feel it return to the beach the way a tide returns, not a wave but a *rising*, the weight coming down on the top of my skull and the pressure building in my ears until I hear my own blood, and it does not go to me this time. It goes to you. It goes to you entire, the whole vast indifferent attention of the Hanged God settling on a barefoot girl in a torn grey dress like a mountain settling on a flower, and I watch your body take it. Your spine goes straight. Your head goes back. Your white eyes open wider, as if there were something to see. And the breath goes out of you in a long low sound that is not pain and is not anything I have a word for, and I know — the way I knew the shield-wall was going to break at Lundenwic a heartbeat before it broke — that he has said *yes*.

And then the rope.

---

There is no rope.

I want that understood with the same clarity as the torch. There is no rope and there is no hand and there is no branch; there is a girl standing on clay in front of a burning stone with her arms out, and then her chin *jerks*. Up and to the side, hard, the way a chin jerks when a noose takes it, and her shoulders come up around her ears and her bleeding hands fly to her throat and claw at *nothing*, at skin, at the bite I left there, at a thing I cannot see that is closing on her windpipe with a force that makes the tendons of her neck stand out like cables, and the sound that comes out of her is the sound a man makes on a gallows. The wet, clicking, airless *hhhk* of a throat being crushed shut.

And her feet leave the ground.

Not far. A foot. The length of my hand. Her bare bloody toes pointing at the frost-white clay and the hem of the ruined dress hanging straight and still and her whole weight *suspended* from the place on her throat where the nothing has her, swinging a hair's width in the dead air the way a hanged man swings, and her hands come down from her throat — I watch her *make* them come down, I watch the fight go out of them not because she has lost it but because she has *chosen* to lose it — and they close into fists at her sides. Bleeding. Trembling. Held.

*Nine nights I hung on the windy tree.*

I move.

I do not decide to. There is no deciding in me; there is only the sight of you choking a foot off the ground with your fists clenched and your face going dark and your white eyes bulging, and my body goes *forward*, my right foot coming up to cross the line of black stones, and three things stop me at once.

Kaleb's arms come around my chest from behind.

Jensen's hand closes on my wrist.

And the wolf turns.

It has not moved from your hip since you stepped to the stone. It has howled and it has watched and it has stood like a carving in the blue light, and now it *turns*, in a single fluid pour of grey muscle, and it is between us. Between me and you. One stride from the edge of the ring with its great head low and its ears flat to its skull and its lips drawn back off teeth as long as my fingers, and the glacier eyes are on mine, and the sound that comes out of its chest is not a growl. It is lower than a growl. It is a *refusal*. It is the sound of a thing that has walked through seventy armed men without a flicker of fear now looking at the one man on the beach it has decided *is* a threat, and the threat is not to it. The threat is to *her*. I am a threat to her. If I cross that line I will kill her — I do not know how I know this, I know it from the wolf's eyes the way I knew it from Jensen's *perhaps* — and the wolf will tear my throat out to stop me, and it will be *right*.

I stop. My chest heaves against Kaleb's arms. I feel him take my weight and hold it, feel his breath hot and ragged against the back of my neck, feel the strength in him that has never once in twenty-one years been turned against me and is turned against me now, and I could break it. I know I could. I am bigger than he is and I have the fury in me and I could break his hold and Jensen's grip both and go through the wolf with my bare hands, and I do not, and the not-doing is the hardest thing I have ever done in my life.

"Brother." Kaleb's voice in my ear. Cracked. Not a joke in it anywhere. "Brother, brother, *no*. She said. She *told* you. *Do not interfere*. You *heard* her."

"She is *dying*."

"She is *hanging*." Jensen. Flat. Exact. Still on one knee, his hand around my wrist like iron, his wet face turned up to mine. "Look at her. Look at her *hands*, Loki. Look at her *feet*." His grip tightens. "This is the *tree*. He is showing her the tree. *Let her hang.*"

And the fire comes for her.

It comes off the pillar. Not a tongue — a *flow*, the blue-white light at the pillar's base simply *spreading*, pouring down off the altar's edge and across the frost-rimed clay like water from a broken dam, silent and cold and shadowless, and it reaches the place beneath your hanging feet and it *rises*.
