He says the last one the way a man says a thing he has been told not to say. "Ott named her. Ott thought it was a joke. Halldis told him it was not a name to put on a hull and he laughed at her, the one time I ever saw him laugh at her, and he named her anyway." He pauses. "They sailed north from the fjord twelve winters past. Around the Kveldfell headland. Bound for Bjarmaland, to open the fur-road that the Sámi would not open for us. A hundred and thirty men. Steinn Ottsson had the Hrímfaxi."

"Steinn —" Sven's voice comes small, from behind. He's seventeen winters old and born the year after.

"The Jarl's half-brother." Jensen's voice is flatter than I have heard it. He has not taken his eyes from the ship. "Younger. The one men said should have had the Seat, if the old Jarl had lived another winter to say so. Ott loved him. It is the only thing I have ever heard a man say that Ott loved. Hákon, one of Ott's trueborn sons favors him with hair black as coal, and that hair has bought Hákon a place at Ott's side for years."

Your face does not change at the mention of your half-brother. But your eyes do and I do not miss them change, and I put it where I will remember it.

"They did not come back." Ketill's voice has gone to almost nothing. "No wreck. No oar. No body on any shore from the Kveldfell to the Dvina. Halldis threw the bones at midwinter and said Rán had them in her hall and the fjord mourned for a season and Ott hung the Serpents' shields in the Great Hall and never once spoke Steinn's name again." His hands on the rail are shaking. "That is Kolbeinn's stem-post. That is the Hrímfaxi. I know her the way I know my own wife's face. I helped carry her mast."

Your silence stretches, heavy with history I do not yet know.

"Loki." I breathe your name. "Something comes. Something *wrong*." I look up at you, amber to green and the runes on my ribs flare suddenly hot and white-blue.

Pain makes me hiss next to you and my hands move to reach for my spine unbidden. The marks pulse hot beneath my skin—searing lines of power I have kept hidden even from myself, inked in a language older than the village that cast me out, older than the wolf-mothers who raised their voices to the moon. They are shadow runes. Blood-bound and dark and heavy with the old gods. It is magic older than even seiðr and it is always hungry. They are the ones that feed the darkest parts of me; the part of me that *enjoys* when men fear me.

"We cannot stay here." I do not plea. I warn, same as Ragnar Lothbrok's seer would warn him in the stories. There is a moment, as the men begin to see me the same as her, and you notice it and I see, even in the heat of this moment, a flare of something like pride in your pale green eyes.

My hand comes and strokes Skuggi. I know what the men see. I know what I look like. Your hand is at my lower back, and I do not pull away. I *lean* into it, using you as an anchor and warmth against the magic that is surging inside me. "We must alter course. Find a different way to your father's hall."

---

Skuggi claws tighten on my forearm, not painful—*urgent*. His obsidian eyes fix on the grey ship, and through the bond I feel Galdur's hackles rise in silver synchrony, his glacier eyes tracking the sealskin figure with a predator's recognition of threat and an older being's understanding there is something *other* inside that threat. I look to you, amber eyes going honey and then going *lighter* than honey, the runes on my ribs and spine burning more and more as the power rises in me. My voice holds steady and I speak what I Sense without hesitation. "There is something there. Something waiting for us along that path. Something wrong and colder than your father's snows." I go to say something else, something for you alone, a plea for you to believe me from a throat that does not know how to plea — 

Then, the order comes from someplace inside me I have never had words for, an instinct older than time that I follow now without question. I have been following such orders since I was old enough to understand the words spoken. From six years old, these instincts and orders have been my guide, and I do not waiver now as I did not waiver then.

Without another word, I throw my right arm up, sending Skuggi into the sky. His massive wings beat with the fury of a thousand war drums as he climbs high above. My hand reaches for yours, *trusting* you in a way I have never once trusted anyone —

"I must do this. I need to See." My eyes meet yours and for one breath, I wait. I will not betray your trust, not now. I made the promise, to ask first, to always involve you — I will not shatter the trust not only you, but eleven-hundred odd men have placed without it being your choice as much as mine. You show me no fear. You only pull me to you, holding me steady, *protecting* my body. "Go. I have you." Simple words you can't know hit me in a place I thought long guarded. I do not hesitate, but I let you see where they hit in my eyes. I let you see what it *means* to me and exactly what I mean to do about it when we survive this.

Then, I slip my skin, into Skuggi to see with his eyes. The color bleeds out, as before, becoming completely white, and no one on the deck breathes when my secret is finally revealed for them all to bear witness to.

---

On the beach, Odin waited to be called. He waited for the gate to open, and for me to bear His price before He reached down with flame and hand and commanded me remade. He does not wait for the gate this time. The moment I open my eyes in Skuggi's skin, the whole of the fleet feels Him, just as before, the Allfather reaching for ME this time — the moment His raven and His daughter become one.

The vision comes with Him. My voice changes, no longer mine. It is HIS and mine in same breath, woven as my hair is woven, thunder and smoke and POWER. Every man of the fleet hears Him as they did on the beach, not just in ear but in soul. This time, it is not just seventy men who bear witness; it is ALL of them.

Just as He laid bare the price with the story of His hanging, the words of the vision come forth with the same thunder and the same truth as the lays of old:

*"A king sits betrayed on a throne of bone and wood, trapped by black, saved by gold."*

*"Two dragons fight on a grave adorned by a field of red heather."*

*"One thousand ships of black oak and serpent skin, manned by echo and smoke, hulls burned in ice-burn."*

*"The Children of the forest dance in halls of redwood long forgotten."*

*"The far north wakes again. What is dead may never die."* 

The vision fades and I gasp as if breaching water, shaking against you as I am forced back into my skin and Skuggi comes back to land on your shoulder. The men around us are utterly pale and they are looking at me with equal parts mixed panic and the particular awe of warriors raised on the stories and seeing the stories made flesh.

I pay it no mind. The vision has ended but the Sight remains, along with the insistence of the runes of omen and war, telling me clear what I must do.

"Let me call the mist." My eyes search yours and a shiver of true fear bleeds into me, making me lean closer to you, seeking your warmth and your touch. "I can cover the fleet and create decoys of shadow they will follow. The sea will answer me — she will keep us off the rocks. But we cannot take the normal route." I look out to the horizon and *feel* what the sea tells me. "We must go west Loki. With haste."

Jensen goes rigid from where he has his eyes trained on the ship and is watching in horror something he has only heard of the old stories emerge. "Impossible..." He breathes, as a wolf the size of Galdur steps beside the sealskin figure; a wolf with thick layers of frost covering its blood-soaked fur from fatal wound made long ago and filmed blue eyes that are dead and should no longer see — yet are glowing with unnatural life. Galdur stands to his full height, lips peeling in absolute fury and hate, putting himself between *both* of us and the undead kin he now sees. Skuggi screams once, a sound of challenge and warning.

For one moment, the entirety of the world seems to hold still. Then, the air and sea are split with the voice of a wolf howl the world forgot how to hear centuries ago, as the dead one sings.

It goes on past where a wolf's breath would end. Past where a man's would. It goes on and on and it does not waver and it does not break and it is not one voice. Under it — I hear it as it climbs, the way you hear the second drum under the first — there are others. Not from the ship; from others in a distance. A chorus of them rising to meet the dead wolf's voice like the drowned coming up to meet a dropped lantern, low and vast and wordless, and I understand — the way I understood the price, all at once and completely — that the world did not forget how to make this sound. The world forgot how to hear it. And it has been going on, in the cold under the cold, the whole time.

Mildrith screams.

Under the forward thwart. A thin high Saxon shriek, and then the Latin comes —  fast, broken, *libera nos a malo, libera nos* — and no one turns.

### Loki

The word *dead* lies on the deck of my ship between Thorolf's boots and mine like a thing thrown down to see who will pick it up.

I do not pick it up. I want that understood before anything else, because the men will tell this part of it as though I stood there weighing the captain's words like a merchant weighing hacksilver, and I did not weigh anything. I heard *Halldis will want her dead* in the voice of a man who rowed across half a mile of iron water to say it, and the white came up in me so fast that it was in my teeth before it was in my mind. It does not start in the chest the way the skalds say rage starts. It starts at the base of the spine, where the shadow-marks start on you, and it goes up the bones of the back one by one like a man climbing a ladder in the dark, and when it reaches the skull it does not roar. It goes very quiet. The wind in the rigging goes away. The slap of the Skarfr's hull against ours goes away. Kaleb's breathing at my right shoulder goes away, and Jensen's at my left, and the low murmur of forty-six men pretending to work goes away, and what is left is Thorolf Ketilsson's throat above the collar of his sealskin jerkin and the place on the left side of it where the great vessel comes up under the red beard, and the distance between that place and the seax on my hip, which is one stride and a turn of the wrist.

He has come onto my deck. He has looked at what is mine. He has told me that my father will *want* it, as though want were a thing a Jarl could do to the Wolf's property without first going through the Wolf, and then he has told me that a one-eyed woman who has never once in nineteen years done what you did on that beach will want it *dead*. And he has said all of it with his feet set and his hands open and his eyes level, the way a man says a thing he has been sent to say and believes, and somewhere in the white place I understand that he is not threatening you. He is warning me. I understand it the way I understand the sea is wet. It makes no difference. The white does not care what a man means. The white cares that a man has put the word *dead* in the air within three paces of you, and that the man is standing on my deck, and that my hand is already on the antler grip and the blade is already a finger's width clear of the sheath, and that Thorolf has seen it and has not stepped back, and that I will have to kill him for not stepping back, and that I do not mind.

Behind me Galdur has stood. I hear his claws on the planking. Skuggi has stopped preening on the sternpost. Kaleb's weight has shifted onto the balls of his feet the way it shifts in the yard when he means to come between me and a man before I have finished deciding, and I know he is going to be too slow, because he is always too slow when it is the white, and he knows it, and he will try anyway, and I love him for it and I will put him on the deck if he touches me.

"The gods will not be in the room. But I will be."

Your voice.

It does not come from behind me where I put you. It comes from my side, from under my arm, from the place against my ribs where you had been tucked since the horn went, and I realize that you did not stay where I put you. You did not stay behind my back when I turned to face the captain. You stepped *with* me, the way a man in the shield-wall steps with the man beside him when the wall moves, so that you were at my side when Thorolf came over the gunwale and you are at my side now, and you have spoken into the white place in a voice so level and so low and so entirely unafraid that the white stops climbing.

It does not go down. I want to be honest. It does not go down. It stops, the way a wave stops at the top before it falls, hanging, and the hanging is worse, because I can feel every ounce of its weight waiting.

Kaleb's head comes round. I feel it more than see it. And Jensen steps forward. Two strides, fast and silent, the way he moves when he has decided a thing before his mind has caught up to it, and he is not stepping toward Thorolf and he is not stepping toward me. He is stepping toward *you*. Putting his lean body between you and whatever is about to come out of me, because he has seen the white more times than any man alive except Kaleb, and he knows that when it comes it does not care what is in its path, and he has decided — my flat, exact, faithful brother — that the god-born will not be in its path. You lift your hand. I see it at the edge of my sight, the written palm turning outward toward him, not hard, not a command, simply *there*, and Jensen stops as though the hand had touched his chest.

And then the hand comes to mine.

Not my hand. My chest. Flat, over the heart, the third time you have put it there, and my chest is not still under it the way it was on the beach. My chest is heaving. I can feel the breath going in and out of me like a bellows, silent and huge, the snarl that has not come out of my mouth living in my ribs instead, and your palm lands on it and rides it the way a hand rides the flank of a horse that has not yet decided whether to bolt. I look down at you. I do not mean to. I mean to throw the hand off. I can feel the throw building in my shoulder, the turn of the body that will send you a step back and clear the space between me and Thorolf's throat, and I look down to see where you will land so that you do not go over the gunwale, and your eyes are waiting for mine.

They are not challenging me. That is the thing that undoes it. I have been challenged my whole life. I know what a challenge looks like in a man's eyes and in a woman's, the lift and the dare and the *try it*, and I know what to do with it, which is to answer it. Your eyes are not doing that. They are quiet. They are as quiet and as steady as the hand on my chest, honey in the grey light with something burning low at the center, and they are not asking me for anything and they are not telling me anything, they are simply *holding*, the way a hand holds a rope that is running out through a block too fast, not to stop it, to slow it, to take some of the weight. And you lean into me. Not against the hand — with it. Your breasts against my chest through the wool of my own tunic, your hips against my thigh, your whole small weight given to me with no armor on it anywhere, as though you were not afraid that the white would take you too, as though you knew, somehow, that it would not.

I blink. I feel it happen. The white does not like it.

"You heard them on the beach." Still low. For me. "I know you did."

I did. *Skjaldmær*. Ásgeir, at the church end of the fan. *Valkyrja*. Old Ketill, with his eyes on the bird.

"*Skjaldmær*," you say, and you say it loud enough that the stern hears it, and you let it fall among the four of us like a stone into a well, and I watch it land on Kaleb's face and on Jensen's and on Thorolf's, and I feel it land on mine hardest of all. "Ragnar's seer did not hide behind him. She stood *next* to him."

My hand is still on the seax. The blade is still a finger clear of the sheath. I do not put it back and I do not draw it, and the not-doing is a thing I can feel in every tendon of my arm.

"I am no soft maid from some fancy hall in need of pampering." Your voice does not rise. It stays where it is, in that low smoke-and-wine place, a woman's voice for a man's ear and no one else's, though everyone can hear it. "You took me off that beach yourself, with your teeth in my throat and your cloak on my shoulders and your claim made before the Allfather himself." Your palm presses. Once. Hard enough that I feel the heel of it against my breastbone. "And you did not take me as a *thrall*, Loki." The fire comes up in your eyes, finally, but it is not for me. "Do not diminish what this is. What we are."

*Diminish.* I hear the word go into me and I do not have a shelf for it either. I had thought I was doing the opposite. I had thought that putting my blade in a man's throat for saying your name near the word *dead* was the largest thing a man could do for a woman, the clearest *mine* there was, and you have just told me, with your hand on my heart in front of my brothers and a sworn captain, that it is small. That it makes you small. That a man who kills for a thing is saying that the thing cannot stand on its own, and that you can, and that you will, and that you would like me to stand beside you while you do it instead of in front.

I do not know what my face does. I know my hand comes off the seax. The blade goes home into the sheath with the soft hiss of oiled leather, and I hear Kaleb let out a breath that he must have been holding since the horn.

You turn your head to Thorolf.

The amber is calm. Not cold — I know what cold looks like in your eyes now, I saw it turn on Mildrith in the mud — but calm, settled, resolute, the eyes of a woman stating the depth of water to a steersman. "I hung for that blessing."

Thorolf's jaw tightens. He had been still before. He goes stiller.

"I burned for the right to choose the man I will stand next to, in a fire that came from nothing mortal, on the beach of a village that spent the whole of my life beating choice out of me." Your tone does not change. Your eyes do. Something in them goes down very deep and comes back up holding a thing I recognize, because I have seen it in polished iron, and it is the thing that a body builds when it has been beaten long enough and decides it will not be beaten again. "*No one* will ever take choice from me again." Your free hand lifts. Not toward Thorolf. Toward me. A small gesture, palm up, the way a woman indicates a thing on a table. "*He* is my choice." The hand moves. Out, past my shoulder, over the stern-platform to the rowing-deck where forty-six men have entirely given up pretending to work, and past them to the gunwale and the grey water and the twenty ships strung out ahead of us in sailing order with their sails bellied. "All of *you* are my choice. And it was heard. And it was *accepted*, because *I* was accepted, and that is why His hand came."

Nobody on the deck breathes. I hear the not-breathing.

"When I See, I See for Loki and the men he commands." Each sentence set down like a stone in a wall, course on course, dry-fitted. "When I fight, it is for Loki and the men who fight at his side. When I heal, when I bless, it will be for Loki and the men who are loyal to *him*." A pause, and a thing goes across your mouth that is almost a smile and is not kind. "Not his silver. It is at their table I will break bread. And it is them I will guard with all I am."

And then you look up at me. Small. Fierce. Your hand still flat on my chest and your chin lifted and the bite blue-black on your throat above the bronze wolf of the clasp, and the look on your face says a thing that I have watched women try to say to me with their mouths for twelve years and never once believed, and I believe it now, because you are not saying it to me. You are saying it to a captain and a ship and a fleet. *Yours*.

And the silence does a thing I do not understand.

It widens. I feel it go out from the stern of the Úlfsvaka the way rings go out from a stone dropped in still water, past the mast and the forward thwart and the wolf-prow and over the gunwale onto the sea, and it does not stop there. I turn my head. I cannot help it. The Skarfr is bumping against our starboard strakes with her oars shipped and her crew at the rail, and every face on her is turned to our stern, and they are not looking the way men look when they are trying to catch a conversation on another ship. They are looking the way men look when they have *heard* it. And past the Skarfr, a long bowshot off our starboard bow, the Járnkjaftr has come up into the wind and is lying with her sail shivering, and I can see Bersi Grimsson on her steering-platform with his arms no longer folded, his whole body turned toward us, and his men behind him the same, and past the Járnkjaftr the Gjallar, and past the Gjallar the low fat shape of the knarr *Breiðr* wallowing at the edge of the line with her sail half-dropped as though someone had let go of the halyard to listen, and on every deck, every one, the faces are turned to the stern of the flagship.

You were not loud. I want that set down. You did not shout. You spoke the way a woman speaks across a hearth, and the woman's voice went out over half a mile of grey water as though the sea had picked it up and carried it ship to ship, the way the Allfather's voice went into every man on the beach at once, everywhere, from no direction. I feel you realize it at the same moment I do. Your hand on my chest goes very still and the fingers spread a hair and I feel a tremor go through them, small and quick, gone before I could name it, and I look down and your face has not changed but your eyes have gone out over the water to the faces on the Járnkjaftr and something is moving behind the amber that you are refusing to look at. The fire gave you back. It did not give you back the same. I do not know how I know that you are thinking it. I know you are thinking it, and I know that you have decided not to, and I put my hand over yours on my chest and hold it there and do not say anything, because there is nothing to say that would not make it larger.

Thorolf studies you.

He takes his time. He is a man who reads water for a living and he reads you the same way, from the bare feet on the planking to the rolled hems of my breeches to the black pelt to the bite to the white braid to the eyes, slowly, the way he would read the color of a sea before committing a keel to it, and I let him. I let him because something has changed in the way he is looking and I want to see what it changes into. The rehearsed thing has gone out of his face. The sent thing. The man who rowed across with Bersi's question in his mouth is not there anymore, and what is there is a man of forty-two winters who has had a god's hand put on his crew and has just heard the woman who called it down tell him why, and is deciding. I watch you hold under it. You do not look away. You do not drop your eyes or lift your chin further or do any of the things a woman does to make herself look braver than she is. You simply stand there and are looked at, and the only sign that it costs you is your hand on my chest, which tightens, slowly, a little more with every breath he does not speak, until your fingers are fisted in the wool over my heart and I can feel your pulse in them.

Then his eyes leave you and come to me. And they stay.

I have been looked at by sworn men before. I know the look a man gives his paymaster, and I know the look a man gives his Jarl, and I know the look a man gives a commander he would follow into a shield-wall he did not expect to walk out of, and they are three different looks and I have seen all three on Thorolf's face over six years. This is none of them. This is a fourth thing, and I have only ever seen it once before, on a hillside above my father's fjord when I was sixteen and had just come back from my first raid with my first prow and my first dead, and my father's old steersman Ívarr came up to me in the dark by the boat-sheds and looked at me like this, and then knelt, and put his hands between mine, and said *the old man will not live forever, boy, and when he goes I would rather be standing behind you than behind anyone he leaves*. Ívarr died at Lundenwic. I have never told anyone what he said. And Thorolf Ketilsson is looking at me with Ívarr's face, on my deck, in front of Kaleb and Jensen and the Allfather's daughter, and it has nothing to do with silver, and he does not need to kneel for me to understand what oath is being sworn behind his eyes, and to whom it is being sworn, and to whom it is not.

My father's name is not in that look anywhere.

"She will get you all *KILLED*."

Mildrith. From under the forward thwart, in a Norse so broken that half the words come out Saxon-shaped, high and cracked and furious, the voice of a girl who was everything in a world of sixty huts and has been nothing for three days and cannot bear it one breath longer. She has come up onto her knees with her bound wrists in front of her and her yellow hair in her face and her blue eyes blazing, and the whole forward deck turns to look at her, and she does not care. "You will lose *everything*. All of you. Everything you have. She — she *ruins*. That is what she *is*. Ask anyone. Ask *my father*."

No one answers her. Hauk, at the forward thwart, puts his boot against her shoulder and pushes her back down without looking at her, the way a man pushes a dog off a bench.

Thorolf does not turn his head. His eyes stay on me.

"She will get him crowned, thrall," he says. Not loud. Not to the girl. To me. "She will give him the north."

The deck holds that in its mouth and does not swallow.

I let it hold. I let it sit there in front of forty-six men and the Skarfr's crew at the rail and Bersi on his steering-platform, because a thing like that, once said, cannot be unsaid, and I want every man who heard it to understand that I heard it too, and did not strike the man who said it, and did not tell him he was wrong. Treason has a sound. I have heard it in my father's hall from men who were dead by morning. This is not quite that sound. It is the sound just before it, the sound of a thought that has not yet been given a name being given one, and I watch it go down the rowing-deck in the faces of my men, man to man, the way the god's hand went, and I see Ketill close his eyes, and I see Torvald look at the deck, and I see Hallr at the steering oar not move at all, and I see Hauk at the forward thwart with his boot on a Saxon girl's shoulder look at me with something in his face that I mark and keep.

"You have said a thing," I tell Thorolf, finally. My voice comes out rough and quiet. "On my deck. In the hearing of my men and yours."

"I have."

"My father has killed men for less than half of it."

"Your father," Thorolf says, "was not on that beach." He holds my eye. "And Bersi asked me to come across and ask you what you meant to do with her at the fjord, and I will go back across and tell him the answer, Wolf, and the answer is that you do not mean to do anything with her. She means to do it with you." Something moves at the corner of his mouth, under the red beard, that on another man would be a smile. "Bersi will not like it. Bersi's daughter is promised into Hákon's household at midwinter, and Bersi has been sweating since dawn yesterday trying to work out which side of the Great Hall that leaves him standing on. That is why it was my face in your stern and not his." He shrugs, a heavy rolling shrug like a hull settling on a swell. "I have no daughters. I have a ship and a crew that stood up straight this morning for the first time in years, and I know whose hand it was."

Hákon. My father's trueborn son by his lawful wife, four years younger than me, with hair black as a crow's wing and a face my father looks at the way he has never once looked at mine. I had not known about Bersi's daughter. I put it away, and I put it away hard, with the knowledge that Thorolf told it to me freely, which is the kind of gift a man gives once and only once, and only to the one he has chosen.

He reaches up to his right ear.

The gold ring there — the heavy plain hoop he took off a Frisian bishop at Dorestad, the one he has worn in that ear for as long as I have known him — comes out with a twist and a small wet sound and a bead of blood where it pulled, and he does not wipe the blood. He holds the ring in his fingers for a moment and looks at it. Then he walks the three paces to the seam in the planking where the stern-platform ends, and he stops at it, exactly at it, his boot-toes on the line and not one hair over, and he crouches and lays the gold ring down among the eleven things — beside Hallr's arm-ring, I see, and he sees it too, and something passes over his face — and stands, and turns, and looks at you.

"I do not do this for gods," he says. "I have never once given a god a thing I could keep." He touches the bleeding ear. "That is for the man you chose. So that he remembers who was the first captain to row across and ask."

He goes back over the gunwale without another word. The Skarfr's oars come down. And as she pulls away from our side, Kaleb lets out a long breath beside me and says, very low and very dry, "I had money on you killing him, brother. Two marks with Torvald. I want it noted that I have just lost two marks on your behalf and I expect to be compensated."

"You bet on me killing a sworn captain."

"I bet on you killing a sworn captain *for saying the word dead near her*, which is not the same thing at all, and which I would have won if she had not —" He stops. He looks at you, under my arm, with your hand still fisted in my tunic, and his face does a thing I have not seen it do. "If she had not *done that*."

Jensen has not moved from where your hand stopped him. He is standing two strides off with his eyes on you and his hand at his throat on the iron hammer, and when he speaks it is the second-tally voice, the one for things that are not counted in silver.

"She was not loud," he says. "I was standing two strides off and she was not loud, and Bersi heard her. I watched him hear her." He touches the hammer. "Perhaps it was the wind."

There is no wind to speak of. The sail has been half-slack since dawn. He knows it, and I know it, and you know it, and none of us says so.

---

The sun goes down red over the starboard quarter, and my blood does not.

I cannot explain it better than that. I have killed men and felt the white drain out of me in the time it takes to wipe a blade. I have fought a whole morning in a shield-wall and been asleep by noon with my head on my shield. But I did not kill Thorolf, and the white did not get what it came up for, and it has been sitting in my spine the whole afternoon like a dog shut out of a hall, scratching. I have done every useless thing a man does with it. I have gone forward and checked the forestay that Torvald checked an hour before. I have taken an oar on the fourth bench for a watch, and the man whose oar it was took one look at my face and found other work. I have cut the throat of the last of the Saxon pigs in the hold for the evening meal myself, with more care than a pig is owed, and held it while it bled out into the bucket with my knee on its ribs, and felt the twitching go out of it under my knee, and that helped for the length of a breath and then did not. Kaleb has stopped talking to me. That is how I know how bad it is. Kaleb talks to me through everything, through rage and grief and fever and drink, and since midafternoon he has looked at me twice and said nothing and gone back to splicing a line that did not need splicing.

So when the sun is half into the sea and the deck has gone red-gold with it, and I am sitting on my sea chest in the stern with my forearms on my knees and my head down, watching a drop of pig's blood dry on the back of my hand and thinking about Bersi's daughter and Hákon's hall and a one-eyed woman's bones, and I feel your hands come down on my shoulders from behind, the first thing that happens in me is that I nearly break your wrist.

I want that set down too. It is true, and the true things are the ones that matter. No one comes at the Wolf's back. Not Kaleb, not Jensen, not Ylva in nine winters of sharing my bed — Ylva learned in the first month to come at me from the front, always, where I could see her hands, after the night she touched my shoulder in the dark and woke up on the floor of the Den with my knee on her chest and my seax at her throat. A man's back is where he dies. It is where the knife goes in the ones who are not killed honestly. Every boy in my father's yards learns it, and the bastard boy who slept in the kennel learned it better than any, because the trueborn boys came for him in the night from behind more times than he could count, and he learned to sleep with his back against a wall and to wake with his hand already moving. And your hands land on my shoulders, both of them, small and warm and sudden, and my right hand is moving before I know I have a right hand, up and back to take the wrist and turn it and bring the body that owns it over my shoulder onto the deck —

And stops. Halfway. Because my hand has closed on your wrist and my body knows the wrist. Knows the size of it. Knows the bones of it, fine as a hawk's leg, because it has held them in a fist on a beach and measured them. And the stopping is so violent that I feel it in my elbow like a jolt from a blade striking stone, and I am sitting there with your wrist in my hand and my arm cocked to throw and my breath gone and my heart slamming, and you have not moved. You have not pulled. You have let me take the wrist, the way you let the stone take your cheek on the beach, and your other hand is still on my shoulder, and you are waiting.

I let go. It takes me a moment. My fingers do not want to open.

"I am sorry," I do not say. I have never once in my life said it and I do not say it now. What I say, rough and low, without turning my head, is, "Do not come at my back."

"I know." Your voice behind me. Quiet. Not frightened. "I watched you all afternoon. I know." And your hand that I let go of comes back to my shoulder, and settles there beside the other, and does not leave. "I am going to anyway."

And your thumbs press in.

Into the thick slab of muscle that runs from the base of my skull out to the point of the shoulder on either side, the trapezius, the oar-muscle, the shield-muscle, the one that has carried every swing and every pull and every hour at the steering oar and every night on watch since I was twelve years old, and that has been, I discover in that moment, a single solid knot of rope from neck to arm for so long that I had forgotten it was ever anything else. Your thumbs find the knot. They find it as though they knew exactly where it was, which they could not, and they press — not gently, you do not do anything gently — and they *hold*. Deep. Unhurried. The heels of your hands bracing on the tops of my shoulders and your thumbs driving into the muscle beside my spine, and the healing-marks on them are warm, I feel the warmth through the wool, the same hearth-heat as on the beach, and the knot under your thumbs does a thing I have never felt muscle do.

It gives.

Not all at once. Not like a rope cut. Like a knot worked loose by fingers that know knots, a strand at a time, the fibers of it parting one from the next under the steady warmth, and as each one goes something goes out of me with it. I do not know what. Something I have been holding so long that it had stopped feeling like holding. My shoulders come down. I feel them come down — a finger's width, then another, then another, sinking away from my ears where they have been living since I cannot remember — and my head goes forward on my neck, and the breath that has been going in and out of me all afternoon like a bellows in a smithy goes long, and slow, and deep, and comes out of me in a sound I did not know I could make, a low rough exhale that is half a groan, and my eyes close.

Your thumbs move. Out along the shoulder, working. In along the spine, working. Up the cords of the neck to the base of the skull and into the hollows there where the skull meets the spine, pressing, circling, and I feel my jaw unclench, I feel it go, the teeth that have been set against each other since Thorolf came over the gunwale coming apart, and the ache in the hinge of the jaw that I did not know was there flooding out into the side of my face like water out of a cut skin.

The white goes.

It does not drain the way it drained when you flinched from me. It does not burn out. It simply *settles*, the way silt settles in a bucket when you stop stirring it, the way a dog shut out of a hall finally stops scratching and lies down against the door and sleeps, and for the first time since the horn went I am not thinking about Thorolf's throat or Hákon's black hair or Halldis's bones or Bersi's daughter. I am not thinking. I am sitting on my sea chest in the red light with my eyes closed and my head bowed and a woman's hands on my back, and the back is where a man dies, and I have let you behind me, and I am not dying. I am — I do not have a word. Jensen would have a word. Jensen's grandmother would have had a song for it. I have nothing but the feeling, and the feeling is that of a ship that has been riding to a single anchor in a gale for three days and nights with every timber in her groaning, and the gale stops, and the anchor-cable goes slack, and every timber in her lets go at once with a long low sigh.

Behind me, a long way off, Kaleb laughs. Very quietly. The real one.

I do not know how long. The sun is gone into the sea when I open my eyes, and the deck has gone from red-gold to the deep blue that comes before dark, and the first stars are out over the starboard bow, and your hands are still on me, slower now, resting more than working, and I reach up without turning and put my hand over one of them and hold it there against my shoulder.

"Where did you learn that." My voice sounds strange to me. Loose. Like a voice that has been let out of a box.

"Nowhere." You do not take your hand away. "I mended men in that village for ten years. Fishermen. They carry their nets the way you carry your shield. Their backs go to stone." A pause. "I never once did this for one of them. I only knew how. I did not know until now that I knew how to do it so that it —" you stop.

"So that it what."

You do not answer. But your thumb moves under my hand, once, across the muscle that is not stone anymore, and I understand that you were going to say a thing and decided not to, and I do not push, because I think I know what the thing was, and I am not ready to hear it said either.

I promised you a braid.

I said it that morning, when you were fighting your hair after the fish and making the sound of pure disgust, and I said it the way a man says a thing to make a woman stop making a sound — *leave it, I will braid it for you, the Viking way, tonight* — and I had not thought about it since, and I think about it now, with my hand over yours on my shoulder and the white gone out of me, and I find that I want to keep the promise more than I have wanted most things.

I stand and turn and put you down on the sea chest where I was sitting. You let me. I go behind you. And I see you know that I have gone behind you, and I see your shoulders draw up a hair — the same thing mine do, the old thing, the back-thing — and then I see you put them down. On purpose. I watch you choose it. And the thing that goes through my chest at the sight of it is so sharp that I have to stand still for a breath before I can lift my hands.

I take the comb from my sea chest.

I have not planned to. I have a comb for my own hair, a good one of walrus ivory that I took off a Frisian merchant, and it is lying on the line among the offerings where someone laid a second one down beside it in the night, and I could use either. My hand goes past the lid to the corner. To the thing wrapped in oilcloth under the spare tunic, that I took out of a dead woman's hand at seven and have used in the dark a thousand times and never once in the light, and never once on any head but my own. It is a small comb, a woman's comb, yellowed bone, missing four teeth, and I hold it in my fist with my thumb over the spine where the scratched mark is, and I do not look at it, and I do not let you see it. I hold it so that the spine is in my palm and only the teeth show. And I begin.

Your hair is a wreck. Salt and wind and a day crushed between your back and my chest have done to it what the sea does to everything, and the god gave it back to you so white and so long and so heavy that the wreck of it is a wreck on a scale I have not seen. I start at the ends. Every woman who ever braided my hair started at the ends — my mother, in the kennel, with this comb, singing — and I start there, a hand's width at a time, holding the hank above the knot so that the pull does not reach your scalp, working the teeth through a strand at a time. It takes a long time. I do not hurry. My hands are a killer's hands, scarred and thick-fingered and calloused from oar and blade, and I am aware of them on your hair the way I am aware of a seax in a crowd, and I make them careful. The hair runs through them like cold water. It smells of the sea and of my furs and under that of you, that before-snow sharpness, and the more of it I work loose the more it shines, even in the blue dusk, even with nothing to shine with, gathering what light there is and holding it. It falls past the edge of the sea chest. It pools on the planking around the legs of it. There is so much of it that I have to gather it over my forearm to keep it off the deck.

And you melt.

I feel it happen under my hands the way you felt the knot go out of my back under yours. The first touch of the comb at the ends and you are still. Upright. Your spine straight against nothing. Then I am at the length of your back, working, and your shoulders begin to come down — not chosen, this time, simply going, the way mine went. Then I am at the crown, the comb running from the part all the way down in long slow strokes now that the knots are out, scalp to waist to the backs of your thighs, and your head goes back. It tips into my hands. Your eyes close. I see them close, from above, the white lashes coming down against the pale skin, and your lips part, and the breath goes out of you in a sound I have not heard you make. Not the growl from the beach. Softer. Lower. The sound a cat makes against a hand, a long shuddering exhale that has a hum at the bottom of it, and your whole body goes loose on the sea chest so that if my hands were not in your hair I think you would slide off it onto the deck, and you do not seem to know you have made the sound, and I do not tell you.

*No one has ever done this for you.* I think it and it goes through me like cold water. Twenty-one years. A hut at the edge of a wood. No mother to sit behind you with a comb and sing. You braided your own hair, every day of your life, with your own written hands, alone, or you did not braid it at all, and no one in that village ever once put their hands on your head except to drag you by it.

I begin to braid.

The Viking way, I called it, and I had meant it for a joke, but I find that I am doing it properly. Not the plain three-strand that thrall-girls wear to keep their hair out of the washing. The one my mother wore, and that she taught me on her own head in the dark so that I could do it for her when her arms were too tired from the day — five strands, crossed and recrossed so that the braid lies flat and tight against the skull at the crown and then runs down the spine in a pattern like the links of a mail-shirt. It is a war-braid. The men wear it into the shield-wall, because a five-strand braid does not come loose and give an enemy a handful to pull. I start it at your crown and I work it down the back of your skull, and my fingers know it, the strands going over and under in a rhythm that is older than me, and somewhere in the back of my mind a woman is singing in Saxon in a corner of a kennel, very low, so that the dogs will not wake, and I hum it. I do not decide to. I hear myself doing it, a low wordless tune under my breath that I have not let out of me in twenty years, and my hands do not stop and the humming does not stop, and you do not open your eyes, and Jensen, at the mast-step, who can hear the tune and who heard my mother sing it when we were six and he came to the kennel at night with bread stolen from his grandmother's table, turns his face away to the sea and does not look back for a long time.

When the braid is at the middle of your back, I stop.

"There is a thing," I say. My voice is lower than I mean it to be. I hear it and I do not bring it back up. "A thing the men do. Before a wall." My hands are still in the braid. "If they have a token — from a woman, from a brother, from a god — they work it into the braid. So that it goes into the fight with them. So that if they die with it, whoever gave it is in the ground with them, and if they live, whoever gave it lived through the fight beside them." I let go of the braid with one hand. I reach past you. To the line. To the eleven things and Thorolf's gold ring that make twelve, lying in the blue dusk on the planking at the seam, and among them, near the end, the hank of red wool thread that someone laid down in the night and did not stay to be thanked for. I take it.

It is good thread. Madder-red, the color of heart's blood when it has had a moment in the air, dyed deep and even by someone who knew what they were doing, finely spun. A thrall-woman's work, or a farm-wife's. Expensive. Whoever laid it down at the line laid down a thing they could not easily replace, and did it in the dark so that no one would see whose hand it came from. I do not know whose. I have thought about it all day. I do not know.

I hold it up where you can see it, over your shoulder.

"Nine turns," I say. "The way you bound the barley." A pause, and I hear my own voice go somewhere it has not been. "If you will have it."

You open your eyes. I see you see the thread. And then I see something happen to your face that I would give every mark of silver on the second tally to see again, and I am not certain I ever will.

Your chest rises. All of it, a long slow lift, the breath going in so deep that I feel your back press against my thighs where I stand behind you, and it does not come out. You hold it. And your eyes — I can see the side of your face, the line of the cheek, the corner of the eye — your eyes *fill*. Not a tear falling. Filling, the amber going glassy and bright and overflowing at the lower lid so that it stands there, held, catching the last blue light, and you do not blink and you do not turn away and you do not hide it from me. You let it stand in your eyes in front of me with your chin lifted and your lips pressed together and your chest full of breath you will not let out, and then you turn your head and look up at me over your shoulder, the way you looked at me when Thorolf came aboard, with your hand on my chest and your whole small weight given to me, and you say one thing.

"Ylva will see this."

I hold your eyes. I hear the name go into me, in your mouth, and I understand — slowly, the way a man understands that the water around his feet is rising — that someone has told you. Kaleb, or Jensen, or a man at the oars with a loose mouth, or Mildrith from under the thwart, who has been learning Norse with her ears pricked for three days. Someone has told you that the Wolf has a woman in his house. That she runs it and warms his bed and wears his keys, and that the whole fjord calls her his wife and he has never once corrected them. And you have known it, and you have let me sit with you in my furs and lie with you in them and put my mouth on your throat and my fingers in your cunt and comb your hair with my dead mother's comb, and you have said nothing, and you are saying it now. Not as a weapon. Not as a question. As a fact. You are telling me what nine turns of red thread in a war-braid will mean when we walk up the hill to the Den, and to whom, and you are telling me with your eyes full and your chest full and your face turned up to mine, and you are not asking me not to. You are asking me if I understand.

I think of Ylva.

I have not thought of her in three days. I want that set down, because it is a true thing and it is not a kind one. Nine winters in my house and three days at sea and I have not once thought of her face. I think of it now. Dark hair, strong hands, a mouth that sets in a line when she is displeased and that has been set in that line more often than not these last two winters. The blue glass beads she asked for. The Saxon girl for the washing, because her hands are cracking. The keys. Four winters ago, the woman who kept my house before her died of a flux in the spring and Ylva took the keys off the peg by the door where they hung and put them on her own belt, and I saw her do it and said nothing, because it was easier than saying something, and she has worn them every day since, and every woman in the fjord knows what it means when a woman wears a man's keys, and I have let them know it. I think of how she goes up the hill to Halldis's house every spring, after the ice goes out, and comes back down with a little leather pouch of something bitter-smelling, and steeps it, and drinks it, and there is never a child in her belly, and I have never once asked her what is in the pouch, or why she goes to Halldis for it and not to the herb-wife by the docks, or what the two of them talk about in that smoky little house with the one-eyed crow on the rafter. I think of all of it in the space of one breath, and I see the shape of a thing in it that I do not like, and I put it away, because it is not for tonight.

And Ylva is from the north. She is from a farm up the fjord where the old ways have not been beaten out of anyone. She will look at a war-braid in a white-haired woman's hair with nine turns of red thread at the binding, and she will know exactly what it means, because nine turns is what you bind a braid with when the man who braided it is telling the world that if you die, he goes into the ground beside you. It is not a thing men do for thralls. It is not a thing men do for a pretty find off a beach. It is a thing a man does once.

I bind the thread.

My hands do not hesitate. I want that known above everything else in this account, because I hesitated over Thorolf's throat and I hesitated over the comb and I have hesitated over more in three days than in the twenty-eight years before them, and over this I do not. I take the end of the braid where I stopped and I finish it down past your waist, five strands, over and under, to the very end of it, a hand from the planking, and I wrap the red thread around it once, and again, and again, nine turns, each one pulled tight and laid flat beside the last so that they make a band of heart's-blood red against the white, and I tie it off with the knot my mother used, the one with the loop tucked under, and I bite the end of the thread off with my teeth.

"Ylva sees everything in my house," I say, with the thread still between my teeth. "That is why I have kept her." I take it out and drop it. "She has seen a great many things in nine winters, elskan. She has seen me drunk and bleeding and weeping over Ívarr. She has seen me fuck three women in one night and throw one of them out in the snow." I lay the finished braid over your shoulder so that it falls down your front between your breasts, white and red against the black pelt, and I keep my hand on it. "She has never once seen me braid a woman's hair."

I bend and put my mouth against your ear.

"Let her see it."

You let the breath out. All of it, at once, shuddering, and a single tear goes over the lower lid and down the side of your face, and you still do not hide it, and I put my thumb on it and take it, and put my thumb in my mouth, the way I did with your blood in the mud. Salt. Only salt. The flint is gone from you everywhere now.

Kaleb, at the mast-step, has stopped pretending to splice. When I look up he is looking at the braid, and his face is very still, and he catches me looking and says, mildly, "Well. I am not going to be the one to tell her."

"Tell who."

"Ylva, brother." He goes back to his splice. "I would sooner tell your father. Your father only kills you."

I do not sleep that night either, and I am glad of it, though I would not have been if I had known what I would watch.

You are asleep in the furs by the time the moon comes up. Not the dead-sleep of the first night — your breath is a woman's breath, even and slow, and you turned twice in my arms before you settled, and once you said something in the old tongue that I did not catch, and your hand found the braid where it lay over your shoulder and held it. Galdur lay down across your feet as he always does. Skuggi went up to the sternpost and put his head under his wing. And I lay on my side behind you with my arm over your waist and my face in the back of your neck, breathing you, and I meant to sleep, I think, for the first time in three nights, and I was close to it, the edges of things going soft — and then you went cold.

I felt it start in your hands. The one I was holding, under my palm on your belly, the warmth going out of it the way warmth goes out of a stone at dusk, slow and steady and complete, and then your arms, and then your shoulders where my chest was against them, until the whole length of you against me was the temperature of a body on a winter morning. Not dead-cold. I know that cold. Not the emptied cold of the beach, either. A cold like a house with the hearth banked, where you know the fire is there under the ash but there is no one tending it. And your breath slowed. I counted it. It went from the slow even breath of sleep to something slower than any sleep I have ever lain beside, a breath every eight heartbeats, then every twelve, so shallow that I had to put my face against your back to feel your ribs move, and your eyes, when I came up on my elbow and looked, were moving under the lids. Fast. Side to side. The way a man's eyes move when he is dreaming of running.

I put my hand on your throat. The pulse was there. Slow. Very slow. I put my mouth by your ear and said your name, and nothing in you answered, not a twitch, not a flicker, as though I had said it to the sternpost.

And Galdur got up.

He rose off your feet in that single pour of grey muscle and stood, and he did not look at you. A wolf that had not left your side by a body-length since the beach, that had stood between me and you with its teeth bared in the fire, that had slept across your feet two nights running — he stood up off your body and did not look at it, and he stretched, long, the forelegs out in front and the spine dipping and the jaws opening in a yawn that showed every tooth in his head, and then he shook himself, nose to tail, and walked away up the deck.

My first thought was that something was wrong with you and the wolf knew it and was leaving. That he had smelled death coming in you the way dogs do and was getting clear of it. My hand closed on your shoulder so hard that if you had been in your body you would have cried out, and you did not cry out, and I came up onto my knees over you with my heart in my teeth, ready to roar for Jensen —

The wolf stopped at the seam in the planking, at the line, and looked back at me.

And I knew.

I cannot say how. I have tried to tell Kaleb and he looks at me as though I have taken a blow to the head, and Jensen nods and does not say anything, which is worse. The wolf looked back at me over its shoulder in the moonlight with its glacier eyes, and the eyes were the same pale ice-blue they have always been, and the thing behind them was not. I have spent three days learning what lives behind those eyes and it is a cold, vast, patient intelligence, an old thing, a thing from the white country that looks at men the way men look at weather. And the thing that looked at me out of them across the moonlit deck was not that. It was quick. It was sharp. It was *amused*, a little, at my face, at the way I had come up onto my knees over your body with my hand white on your shoulder, and under the amusement it was something else, something softer, that looked at me the way you had looked at me over your shoulder with the red thread held up, and then it turned away and went up the deck, and the walk was wrong. It was still a wolf's walk, that liquid hip-rolling pour, but it was a wolf's walk being done by something that was *enjoying* it. Something that was stretching into it, testing it, the way a woman tests a new pair of shoes on a smooth floor, and I knelt there over the cold slow body in my furs with my hand on its shoulder and I understood that I was holding a house with no one in it, and that the one who lived in it had gone out for a walk on four legs, and I had never in my life been so afraid.

I want to be honest about the fear. It was not the fear of the beach, the fear that you would die. It was older than that. It was the fear I had when I was seven and woke in the kennel in the middle of the night with my mother's body beside me gone cold and the dogs whining, and I did not understand yet what it meant, only that she was there and she was not there, and that I was alone with a thing that had her shape. You were there and you were not there. Your shape was under my hand, breathing every twelve heartbeats, and you were walking up my deck in a wolf's skin, and if a man came aft in the dark — if a man crossed the line and put his hand on the shape — there would be no one in it to wake. No one to fight. You had left your body in the furs at the stern of a ship full of men who, three days ago, would have raped it in a sheep-pen without a second look, and you had left it there because I was lying beside it.

I lay back down. I pulled you in against me, the cold weight of you, and I put my arm over you and my hand flat on your breastbone where I could feel the slow heart, and I put my seax, drawn, in the furs by my other hand, and I watched.

The wolf walked the whole ship. Stem to stern, slowly, as though it had all the night in the world. It stopped at every man on watch. Ketill, at the forward thwart, with his back against the gunwale and his eyes half-closed — the wolf came up beside him and stood, and Ketill opened his eyes and found a dire-wolf out of the white country a hand's width from his face, and I saw him stop breathing, and I saw the wolf look at him for a long time, the old man with the bent back that had been made straight, and then lower its head and push its muzzle once, briefly, against his chest, against the place where his bronze hammer had hung on its cord until he laid it down at the line, and move on. Ketill sat there afterward with his hand on his chest where the hammer was not, for the rest of the watch.

It sat with Jensen. That one I watched with my whole body, because Jensen was awake, Jensen is always awake, on the second thwart with his back to the gunwale and his eyes on the black water, and the wolf came and sat down beside him. Not lay. Sat, on its haunches, shoulder to shoulder with him, the two of them facing the same way, looking at the same dark sea, and Jensen did not startle. He did not look at it. He only went very still, and then, after a long time, his hand came up from his knee, slowly, and stopped a finger's width from the grey fur, and waited. And the wolf leaned. A hair. Into the hand. And Jensen's fingers went into the ruff at the wolf's shoulder and stayed there, and the two of them sat like that watching the sea for the length of a full watch, and Jensen's lips moved, once or twice, and I could not hear what he said, and the wolf's ears turned to him each time he said it.

Kaleb fed it. I watched that too, and I will never tell him I watched, because he would put me over the side. He was on the steering oar for the middle watch, relieving Hallr, who had finally gone down to sleep at the foot of the sternpost three paces from my furs with his grandson curled against his back, and the wolf came up the steering-platform and stood beside him, and Kaleb looked down at it for a long moment and said, very softly, so softly that I only caught it because the wind was right, "You are not him, are you." And the wolf looked up at him. And Kaleb's face did a thing in the moonlight, and he reached into the bag at his belt where he keeps the strips of dried mutton he eats on watch, and took one out, and held it down on his flat palm, and the wolf took it — not snapped, *took*, delicately, with the front teeth, the way a woman takes a sweetmeat off a plate — and Kaleb said, "Do not tell him I did that," and the wolf's tail moved once against the planking.

The boy Sven, at the forward thwart, who has flattened himself against the gunwale every time Galdur has passed him for three days, who sat at the oars on the beach and wet himself when the wolf came out of the smoke and has been mocked for it by every man on the bench since — the wolf came to him last. Lay down in front of him, on the deck, on its belly, with its great head on its forepaws and its eyes turned up to him, and stayed there, and waited, the way you would wait for a frightened dog. It took the boy a long time. Long enough that the moon moved a hand's width across the sky. And then his hand came out, shaking so hard I could see it shake from the stern, and touched the wolf's ear, and the wolf did not move, and the boy's hand went from the ear into the fur behind it and began, very slowly, to scratch, and his face in the moonlight went from terror to a kind of wonder that made him look about nine years old, and the wolf closed its eyes.

I know this ship the way I know my own body, and I feel her go from a ship carrying men home to a ship carrying men to a fight in the space of a single shout, the way a dog's hackles go up before the growl comes, and I take my hand off your jaw.

I do not take the rest of me off you. I am a wall at your side. I can feel the heat coming off my own body and I know you can feel it too. My hand goes out and finds the rail where it rises to the sternpost, and closes on it so hard that I feel the grain of the oak through the callus, and I look.

I do not know her.

That is the thing that goes through me first and coldest. I know every ship on this coast. I know every Jarl's prow from the Danish marches to the Kveldfell and every trader's hull that puts in at Kaupang and every pirate's lean grey keel that hunts the islands, and I know them by the cut of the sail and the rake of the stem and the set of the shields along the gunwale, the way a man knows faces in a crowd. I know my father's ships. I know the Danish ships. I know the Frisian cogs and the Saxon hulks and the little skin-boats of the Irish. And I look out at the ship coming up out of the haze on our starboard quarter, and I do not know her, and that is not a thing that has happened to me since I was sixteen.

"Unmarked." Kaleb. He has come aft, fast, with Jensen a step behind him, and he stops at my other side and puts his hand up to shade his eyes and looks, and his voice is tight. "Longship. Lean-built. Old cut — look at the sheer, brother, look how high she comes at the ends, nobody has built like that since our fathers. She rides low. Full of men or full of water, I cannot tell which from here." A pause. "If it is water, she should be on the bottom."

You have moved to the rail beside him. The pelt loose around your shoulders, your hands on the oak, the braid swinging. And the haze parts for her.

It does not part the way haze parts for a ship. It does not thin around her as she comes through it. It *opens*, slowly, reluctantly, the way a curtain opens when someone on the other side pulls it, and she comes out of it into the thin white sunlight and I see her clearly for the first time, and the first clear look at her goes into my belly like cold water.

She is lean, as Kaleb said. Lean and long and low, her strakes gone grey and silver-pale in the way of old wood that has been in salt water a long time without tar, so pale that she looks almost white in the haze-light, like driftwood, like bone. Her sail is up. It is grey too, the red and blue stripes faded out of it until they are only a slightly darker grey against the lighter, and it is torn in two places, long rents from the yard down to the foot, and the torn edges are not fluttering. That is the thing I see second. The torn edges of a sail flutter. In any wind, in the lightest breath of air, a rent sail flutters along its edges like a flag. These do not move. They hang. And the sail itself is full — bellied out, round and taut, the way a sail is full in a good stiff breeze — and there is no good stiff breeze. There is barely any wind at all. Our own sail has been half-slack all morning. And the grey ship's sail is full and round and taut and still, as though it were carved out of stone, and it is full *toward* us. Toward the wind. Bellied out in the wrong direction, with the curve of it facing into the faint breeze instead of away, the way no sail has ever filled on any sea since men first hung cloth from a yard.

Kaleb has seen it. I hear his breath stop.

Her prow is a dragon's — not a wolf's, like ours, not the plain stem of a trader. A dragon's, carved high and arching, and the dragon is made of serpents. Two of them, interlocking, their bodies twisting around each other up the whole length of the stem-post in loops and knots so fine I can see them from here, and at the top their two heads come apart and face out, one to either side, jaws open. It is beautiful work. It is old work. And I know it. I know I have seen it before, somewhere, a long time ago, and I cannot reach where.

And her oars. Her oars are rising and falling. And they are wrong. Not out of time with each other — a crew out of time looks ragged, sloppy, a stroke here and a stroke there, a man catching a crab. This is not that. Each oar is rising and falling at its own pace, its own rhythm, as though every man at every oar were rowing to a different drum that only he could hear, and none of them is wrong and none of them is right and the whole hull is moving through the water at a steady, impossible speed that no crew rowing like that could give her. Hurried. Desperate. Or made to look so. And her rails are crowded. Dark shapes along them, shoulder to shoulder, too many for a trading crew, too few for a war-band, standing there. Not moving. Not shouting. Not doing any of the things a crew does when it raises a strange fleet.

And at her prow, a single figure.

It stands just behind the serpents' heads, on the forward platform, wrapped from head to foot in a grey hide. Sealskin, I think, from the sheen of it. The hood is up and drawn close and I cannot see a face. I cannot tell if it is a man or a woman. I cannot tell anything about it except that it is standing, and that the way it stands is wrong too. A man on a forward platform in any kind of sea moves with it. His knees bend and straighten, his body sways with the roll, he puts a hand on the stem-post when the bow drops. The grey ship is pitching in the swell, I can see her bow lift and fall, and the figure does not move. It stands rooted. Perfectly still. As though the deck were rolling under it by permission, and it had not given the deck permission to move it.

"Reavers," Kaleb breathes. He is close enough that I can smell the mead from his morning on his breath. "Or some small Jarl who means to test his reach against the Wolf." He does not believe it. I can hear him not believe it.

I do not answer him. My jaw is tight. My hand drifts toward the seax on my hip, the short one, the one you cut your palms with, and stops. I make it stop. I want to draw. Every part of me that has ever stood on a deck and looked at a strange ship coming up wants to draw, and I do not, because I am reading. The set of her sail. The angle of her approach — not toward us, not quite, but along us, holding a course a long bowshot off our quarter that will run her up past our beam and on toward the head of the fleet. The figure at her prow. And the thing that is sitting cold in my belly that has no name, that says *you do not draw on this. You do not draw on this because you do not know what a blade does to it.*

You are reading too. I can feel it beside me. You have gone very still at the rail and your hands are white on the oak and your head is tilted a hair, the way the raven's head tilts when it is listening to something I cannot hear. And I see — I am watching you closely enough now that I see it — the skin at the back of your neck, under the braid, where the shadow-marks start, go to gooseflesh.

"Who flies no colors in your father's waters?" You say it to the sea. Your voice is low, and the faint salt breeze takes it and throws it back smaller, and you do not seem to notice. "Who dares approach a wolf-fleet without greeting?"

It is not I who answers you.

"*Hrímfaxi*."

Ketill. Behind us. At the starboard rail of the rowing-deck, at the stern end of it, with his two hands on the gunwale and his eyes on the serpents, and his face. His face has gone the color of the grey ship. "Frost-Mane. For the horse that pulls the night." His voice does not rise. It is the voice of a man saying something he has said to himself in the dark many times. "The second was the *Skinfaxi*. Shining-Mane. For the day." A pause. "The third was the *Naglfar*."

He says the last one the way a man says a thing he has been told not to say. Very quietly. With his eyes closing for an instant as it leaves him, and his hand coming up from the rail to his chest, to the place where his bronze hammer hung on its cord for forty years until he laid it down at the line two nights past, and finding nothing there, and staying there anyway.

"Ott named her." He opens his eyes. "Ott thought it was a joke. He had *Hrímfaxi* and *Skinfaxi*, night and day, and Kolbeinn the shipwright asked what the third should be, and Ott laughed and said the third should be the ship that sails at the end of everything, because his fleet would sail to the end of the world and back. *Naglfar*." He swallows. "The dead men's ship. The one that is made from the nails of the dead, that Hrym steers out of the east when the wolf breaks his chain. Halldis told him it was not a name to put on a hull. She stood up in the Hall in front of everyone and told him it was a calling and not a name, and he laughed at her." Ketill's hands are shaking on the rail. "The one time. The one time in nineteen years I ever saw him laugh at her. And he named her anyway, and she stood in the Hall and went white and sat down and did not say another word, and two weeks after, they sailed."

"Sailed where?" It is Sven. He has come aft. I did not see him. He is standing a little behind Ketill with his young face gone pale and his eyes on the grey ship and his voice coming out small, seventeen years old and the son of a man I knew once, a man who pulled an oar on a ship with a serpent prow and did not come home. "Ketill. Sailed *where*?"

"North." Ketill does not look at him. "Twelve winters past. Around the Kveldfell headland by sea, because Ott had sworn no man of his would go over it by land again, not after — not after the first time." He does not say what the first time was. He does not need to. Every man on this deck old enough to have heard it knows. *Thirty men over the Kveldfell. Nineteen came back. The Jarl never speaks of the eleven.* "Bound for Bjarmaland, to open the fur-road that the Sámi would not open for us. A hundred and thirty men in three hulls. The best crews in the fjord. Steinn Ottsson had the *Hrímfaxi*."

"Steinn —" Sven again. His voice cracks.

"Ottsson." Jensen says it. Flatter than I have ever heard him, flatter than the count, a voice with all the life pressed out of it. He has not taken his eyes off the grey ship. "Son of Ott the Elder, the old Jarl, the same as the Jarl that sits in the Seat now. Half-brother. Younger by eleven years, from the old Jarl's second wife." His hand is on his hammer. "Men said Steinn should have had the Seat. They said it under their breath, at the far end of the Hall, after the mead. That if the old Jarl had lived one more winter he would have named Steinn over our Ott, because Steinn was the better man, and everyone knew it, and our Ott knew it best of all." A pause. "And our Ott loved him. That is the only thing I have ever heard any man say that the Jarl loved. Not his wives. Not his sons. Steinn. He gave Steinn the best ship in the fleet and the best crew and the road to Bjarmaland, which would have made him the richest man in the north after the Jarl himself." Another pause. Jensen's eyes come off the grey ship, for the first time, and go to me. "Hákon favors him. Steinn had hair black as coal. The old men say Hákon is Steinn come again. That is half of why Hákon sits where he sits."

I keep my face where it is.

I want that understood. I keep my face exactly where it is, because there are men on this deck and you are beside me and I will not let one of them see what the name does. Hákon, who sits at my father's left hand while I sit at his right, and who my father looks at — I have watched him look at Hákon for twenty years, across the Hall, across the yards, with a thing in his face that he has never once turned on me, and I have told myself all those years that it was because Hákon was trueborn and I was a thrall's whelp, and I have built a life on that telling. And I am standing on my own deck being told by Jensen, in front of you, that it is not that. That it is the hair. That my father looks at Hákon and sees a dead brother he loved, and looks at me and sees — what. A Saxon length of bone. A thrall's green eyes. Nothing he ever loved.

My face does not change. But I feel you look at me. I feel your eyes come off the grey ship and find my face and stay there, and I know that whatever my face is not doing, my eyes are, and I know that you see it, and that you are putting it somewhere. I do not look at you. If I look at you I do not know what will come out.

"They did not come back." Ketill's voice has gone to almost nothing. "No wreck. No oar. No body washed up on any shore from the Kveldfell to the mouth of the Dvina. Nothing. The Sámi said they never saw them pass. The Bjarmians said they never came. Halldis threw the bones at midwinter and said Rán had them in her hall under the sea, all hundred and thirty, feasting, and the fjord mourned for a season, and Ott hung the serpent ships' shields on the wall of the Great Hall behind the Seat, and he never once spoke Steinn's name again." His hands are white on the rail. "I was to sail with them."

The deck goes still around him.

"I was to have an oar on the *Skinfaxi*." He says it to the sea. "I had my sea chest on the dock. And the night before they sailed I took a fever, a bad one, burning, and I could not stand, and Halldis came down from her house herself and brought me a draught for it, and I slept two days, and when I woke they were gone." His mouth works. "I have thanked her for that draught every Jól for twelve years. I have sat at her hearth and drunk her ale and thanked her." He stops. His face does something. "I had never once been sick a day in my life before that night. Not once. Not since."

Nobody says anything. I put it away. I put it away beside the crow and the pouch Ylva brings down the hill every spring and the bones that said Rán, and the place behind my teeth is so crowded now that I can feel it pressing on the inside of my skull.

"That is Kolbeinn's stem-post." Ketill lifts one shaking hand from the rail and points. "The serpents. He carved them with his own hands the winter before. I watched him do it. He used to say the two serpents were Ott and Steinn, wound together so tight that no one could tell where one ended and the other began, and the two heads looked two ways so that between them they could see everything coming." His voice breaks. "That is the *Hrímfaxi*. I know her the way I know my own wife's face. I helped carry her mast down to the water."

The grey ship comes on.

Silence. My silence. I stand at the rail with my hand on the oak and the serpents coming up along our quarter a long bowshot off and I do not say anything, because there is nothing in me to say. I am thinking — slowly, clearly, coldly, the way I think when the shield-walls are a hundred paces apart and closing — that my father has lost men to the north twice. Once over the Kveldfell by land, for a pelt, when he was young. Once around it by sea, for furs, when he was not. And both times something kept what he sent, and both times he came home and did not speak of it, and both times Halldis was there to tell the fjord what had happened and why, and both times what she told them was wrong. And I am thinking that a ship which has been at the bottom of Rán's hall for twelve years is coming up along my quarter with a full sail and no wind, and that my father's dead brother had the steering of it, and that there are dark shapes along its rail, a hundred and thirty of them perhaps, standing very still. And I am thinking of you on the beach saying *Hroptr* and Jensen saying *carving it is calling it*, and Ketill saying *Halldis told him it was a calling and not a name*. And I am thinking that somewhere in the north, twelve years ago, a ship named for the vessel of the dead sailed into the white country, and the white country *answered*.

"Loki."

Your voice. You breathe my name. I turn my head, finally, and look down at you, and you are looking up at me, and your face is the color of the grey ship. "Something comes." Not loud. For me. "Something *wrong*."

And the marks on your ribs flare.

I see it through the wool of my own tunic on you. A band of light beneath your breasts, under the cloth, going up white-blue so suddenly and so fiercely that the weave of the wool shows dark against it, the war-runes, the pewter-gold ones I saw in the dawn light the morning I put my hands on you — gone to the color of the pillar on the beach — and you hiss. A sharp indrawn sound through your teeth, pain, and your body jerks beside me, and your hands come off the rail and go *back*. Behind you. Reaching for your own spine, unbidden, the way a man reaches for an arrow that has gone into his back, both hands clawing at the air behind your shoulders, and I see the braid swing and I see, at the nape of your neck, above the collar of my tunic, where the shadow-marks begin, the skin go *dark*. Not shadow. Light. Black light, the blue-black of the fire that burned you, coming up through the skin in the shape of the knotted ladder, and the gooseflesh is standing up all down the back of your neck like the hackles on the wolf.

I catch your hands. Both of them, before they reach your back, and I hold them, and I bring you in against my chest with your back to me so that my body is between you and the deck, and I feel the heat of your spine through the pelt and the wool against my ribs. It is burning. Not warm, not the hearth-heat. *Burning*, like a coal through cloth, so hot that I flinch from it and do not let go.

"We cannot stay here."

You say it into the air in front of you, to the sea and the grey ship, with your hands in mine and my arms around you and your spine burning against my chest. You are not pleading. That is what I hear. I have heard women plead and there is a thing in the voice, a reaching, a hand held out. There is no hand in your voice. It is a warning. Flat and certain and cold, the voice of a woman reading water to a steersman, the voice of a seer telling her king what is in the shore before he puts a keel on it, and I hear it the way the men around us hear it, and I feel them hear it, and I feel the deck change.

They are looking at you. Not the way they looked at you on the beach, with terror, and not the way they looked at you this morning, with welcome. Ketill has turned from the rail. Torvald has turned. Kaleb, at my other side, is looking down at you with his face gone still, and Hallr at the oar, and Brandr, and Sven, and Ásgeir up at the bow with his arm still around the stem-post — all of them, the whole deck, looking at the white-haired woman with the burning spine in the Wolf's arms and listening to her tell the Wolf what to do. And there is a thing in their faces that I know from every hall I have ever sat in on a winter night when an old man told the old stories. It is the look men give a story when it stands up off the hearth and walks.

*Ragnar's seer did not hide behind him.*

I feel it come up in me. Through everything — through the cold in my belly and Hákon's black hair and the burning of your spine against my chest — I feel it come up. Pride. Hot and fierce and entirely out of place. I have never once in my life been proud of a woman. I have been pleased with them, satisfied with them, fond of them, and I have never once stood on a deck and watched my men look at a woman of mine the way men look at a thing out of a saga and felt my chest go tight with it. It is in my face. I know it is. I see you see it — your head turns a hair against my chest, and your eyes come up, and find my face, and something goes across yours that is almost a smile, even now, even with the grey ship coming up and your spine on fire.

Your hand comes out of mine and goes to Skuggi on my shoulder. Strokes down his back, once, the way you did on the beach, and the chittering rattle in his chest does not stop but it changes, goes lower, steadier. And your other hand stays in mine. And you lean back into me. Into my chest, against the arm I have around you, letting me take your weight, and I feel the burning in your spine against my ribs and I do not flinch from it this time. You are using me. I understand it. As an anchor, as a wall, as warmth against whatever is rising in you, and you are not hiding it, and the men can see it. My hand comes to the small of your back, between us, flat against the burning, and you do not pull away.

"We must alter course." Steady. "Find a different way to your father's hall."

The raven's talons tighten on my shoulder. Not painful. Urgent. And through my chest, where your back is against me, I feel something go through you that is not you. A rising. A bristling. The wolf. I look down and Galdur is on his feet at our knees with every hair on his body standing, a silver ridge from his skull to the root of his tail, and his eyes are on the grey ship. On the figure at the prow. And the look in them is not the look he gave Osric or Hauk or the smith's widow. It is not the look of a predator measuring a threat. It is older than that. It is the look of a thing that knows exactly what it is looking at, and has known it for a very long time, and hates it with a hatred that is older than men.

You turn your face up to me.

And your eyes are not honey. They are going lighter than honey. Paler, the amber thinning out toward something like the color of the pillar on the beach, the color of the war-marks under the wool, and the burning in your spine against my chest is climbing, and your voice when it comes is steady and does not hesitate.

"There is something there." You say it to me and only me. "Something waiting for us along that path. Something wrong. And colder than your father's snows." You open your mouth again. I see something come up behind your eyes — something for me alone, something you are reaching for, something that has the shape of a plea in it, and I watch you not know how to make it. I watch a throat that has never once in twenty-one years begged anyone for anything try to find the shape of *believe me*.

And then it goes out of your face. I watch it go. A thing comes into your eyes instead — not yours. Not the god's, either, not the vast thing from the beach. Something between. An order, I would call it, if I had to call it anything. A thing you are hearing from somewhere inside yourself and following without question, the way I follow the white, the way a dog follows a scent. I see it come. I see you hear it. I see you obey.

Your right arm goes up.

The one I am not holding. Up, straight, fast, flung toward the sky, and Skuggi goes off my shoulder with a crack of wings that I feel in my face like a slap. Up. Not circling — climbing, straight up, beating so hard that I hear each stroke of the wings like a war drum, *boom, boom, boom*, the great black shape of him going up into the white haze-light until he is a mote against the sky and then above it.

And your hand comes to mine.

The left one. It closes on my hand where it rests on your hip, the healed palm against my scarred one, and holds. *Trusting*. That is what the grip says. I have felt men grip my hand in a hundred ways — in oath, in greeting, in challenge, in death — and I have never once felt a hand close on mine like this. Like a woman going over a cliff closes her hand on a rope.

"I must do this." Your eyes on mine. Pale. Paler. "I need to See."

And you wait.

You wait. One breath. With your hand on mine and your eyes on my face and the raven climbing into the sky and the wolf bristling at our knees and the grey ship coming up along our quarter with its full sail and its still crew, you *wait*. For me. You promised me this morning in the furs and you are keeping it. You are asking. You could have gone — I see that you could have, I see the thing in you that wants to go so badly that your whole body is straining toward the sky after the raven — and you are standing at the rail of my ship with your hand on mine waiting for my word, and you are doing it in front of forty-six men who will remember it, so that every one of them understands that the god-born goes where the Wolf allows. Not because the Wolf owns her. Because she chose to let him.

I pull you to me. Both arms around you, your back against my chest, the burning of your spine against my heart, and I set my feet on the deck the way I set them in a shield-wall, wide and braced, and I put my mouth against your ear.

"Go." Low. Rough. The truest thing I have. "I have you."

Something happens in your face.

I see it from above and to the side, the curve of your cheek and the corner of your eye, and I see the words go in. I see them go into a place I did not know they could reach, a place under everything, under the stone and the fire and the god. Your lips part. Your eyes fill. Not the way they filled with the red thread. Something deeper and stranger and more naked than that, and you let me see it. You turn your head on my chest and look up at me with it in your eyes, and you let me see exactly where those words have gone in you and exactly what they have done, and there is something under it, behind it, dark and hot and certain, that tells me with no words at all what you mean to do about it when we live through this.

And then your eyes go white.

---

The color bleeds out of them the way I watched it bleed out on the beach. Not slowly. From the center outward, the pale amber going paler and paler until it is milk, until it is the flat lightless white of a boiled fish's eye, of the dead eye in Halldis's face, and the white spreads to the edges and there is nothing left of you in them at all. And your body goes slack in my arms.

Not the dead-weight of the beach. Not quite. The cold weight of last night. The hearth banked. The house with no one in it. I feel it go out of you all at once — the burning in your spine dropping from a coal to a stone, your hand on mine loosening and sliding, your head lolling back against my collarbone with your white eyes open and staring up past my face at nothing — and I take your weight. I lock my arms around you. I bend my knees to take it, the way you take the weight of a wounded man, and I hold you upright against the stern rail with your bare feet half-off the planking, and I look up.

The raven is a black point against the white sky, high, higher than any raven flies, and it is not moving the way a bird moves. It is turning. Slowly. A long wide circle over the fleet and the grey ship and the sea between, and every few beats of the circle its head turns, and I know — I know the way I knew the wolf last night — that it is looking at the grey ship, and that you are looking with it.

No one on the deck breathes.

I feel them not breathe. Forty-six men who have heard every story there is about the old ones, the ones who could leave their skin and walk in a beast's — *hamrammr*, the old men say, the shape-strong, or *hamhleypa*, the skin-leaper, said with a hand on the hammer. The stories where they make kings. The stories where they eat children. And every man of them is standing on the deck of the Úlfsvaka looking at a woman hanging slack in the Wolf's arms with white eyes staring at nothing, and at a raven turning slow circles in the sky above them, and understanding.

Torvald says *hamhleypa* under his breath, and the man beside him strikes him in the mouth.

And then He comes.

On the beach, I understood afterward, the Allfather waited. He waited for the stone to be lit and the blood to be given and the gate to be opened, he waited until you had asked three times and hung for the asking, and then He turned His face. That is how it is done. That is how it has always been done, in every story. The gods are asked and the gods answer and the asking has a price.

He does not wait.

The moment your eyes go white, the moment the raven turns its head toward the grey ship, I feel Him. Not turning His face. *Reaching*. Down, out of the sky, the way a man reaches into a stream to grab a fish he has seen — fast, sure, deliberate — and the regard comes down on the fleet like a hand slapped flat on a table, and it is not like the beach. On the beach it was vast and slow and indifferent, a mountain settling. This is urgent. This is the regard of a thing that has *seen something* and wants you to see it too, and it comes down through the top of my skull and the length of my spine and out through my boots into the deck, and through the deck into the keel my father's first ship gave me, and through the keel into the sea, and through the sea — I feel it go — into every hull on the water. Every one. I feel it the way you feel the swell lift a whole fleet at once. Twenty-five hulls. Eleven hundred men. And I hear them. Across the water, from the Skarfr and the Járnkjaftr and the Gjallar and the wallowing knarrs, a sound, low and terrible, eleven hundred men drawing in their breath at once.

The hand on the back of my neck closes.

It has been there since the beach. Warm. A palm resting. It closes now, the fingers coming round to the sides of my throat, not choking, *holding*, the grip a man uses on his son's neck when he wants the boy to look at the thing in front of him and not look away, and I do not look away.

And your mouth opens.

Your head is still lolled back against my collarbone and your white eyes are still staring up at nothing, and your lips move, and what comes out of them is your voice. It is your voice, the low smoke-and-wine alto, I would know it anywhere. And it is not. It is woven. I have no other word. It is your voice and His voice in the same breath, braided together the way I braided your hair, five strands over and under so tight that you cannot see where one ends and the other begins — your alto on top and under it the thing from the beach, the roots of the mountains speaking, the thunder that does not come from a direction — and it does not come out of your throat. It comes out of the air. It comes out of the deck. It comes out of the sea and goes into every man on every hull on the water the way the voice went into seventy men on the beach. Not in the ear. In the soul. In the marrow. I hear it with my bones.

And I understand, at the first word, that this is not a story He is telling. On the beach He told the price — the tree, the spear, the runes. This is not the past. This is what is coming.

*"A king sits betrayed on a throne of bone and wood."*

My father.

I see it before I have decided to see it. The Great Seat, in the Great Hall, at the head of the long hearth. Oak, black with age, the back of it carved with the Midgard serpent biting its own tail, and the two arms of it made from the jawbones of a whale my grandfather took off the Hálogaland coast, white and curved and as long as a man, so that a man sitting in it sits between the jaws of a sea-beast. Bone and wood. I have seen my father sit in it every day of my life. And the word *betrayed* goes into me like a seax under the ribs, and I do not know who. I do not know who. I have six sworn captains whose silver goes through a sinew cord against Jensen's heart before it goes through my father's scales, and a captain who just took a gold ring out of his ear in my stern, and the word goes in and I do not know if it is about me.

*"Trapped by black. Saved by gold."*

Black. Hákon's hair, black as coal, black as Steinn's. Halldis's hood — the black lambskin hood she wears over her blue völva's cloak, lined with white catskin, the hood the old völur wore in the sagas, that she puts on before she throws the bones. Black. The black of the night-stone. The black of the shadow-marks on your spine. I do not know which. My mind goes to all of them at once and cannot choose. And gold — silver is what Ott counts and gold is what saves, and I think of the Miklagarð arm-ring in the oilcloth in my sea chest with the mark inside it that I have not shown anyone, and I think of the twenty-two solidi in Edrik's chest, and I feel Jensen's eyes. I feel them on me from where he stands at the rail. And when I look at him, his eyes are not on my face. They are on my hair. On the top of my head, where the sun is coming through the haze and finding the ash-blonde, and I know what color ash-blonde goes in the sun. I have seen it in polished iron.

Jensen looks away. He does not say anything.

*"Two dragons fight on a grave adorned by a field of red heather."*

The howe.

Behind the Great Hall, on the shoulder of hill above the fjord, past Halldis's stone where she kills pigs, there is a mound. The grave-mound of Ott the Elder. My grandfather. Steinn's father and my father's. Raised in the year of his death, thirty winters past, so tall that a man standing at the bottom cannot see the top, and covered — every inch of it, the whole long green swell of it — in heather. The old Jarl's thralls planted it. Every autumn, when the first frost comes, the heather on the old Jarl's howe goes red. Not purple, like the heather on the fells. Red. Blood-red, the deep rusted crimson of a wound three days old, so that from the fjord the howe looks like a thing that has been bleeding. I have looked at it every autumn of my life. And two dragons fight on it. Two sons. My father had two sons he acknowledged and one he called bastard with pride, and I think of Hákon's black hair, and I think of myself standing on the red heather of my grandfather's grave with a seax in each hand, and I think of my brother's face across from me, and I feel nothing. I want that in the account. I feel nothing, and the nothing is cold, and the nothing is very calm, and the nothing has been waiting a long time.

*"One thousand ships of black oak and serpent skin."*

The haze to the north moves.

*"Manned by echo and smoke."*

*"Hulls burned in ice-burn."*

I look at the grey ship. I look at her pale strakes, gone white like driftwood, like bone, like a thing that has been in salt water a long time and then in cold — cold so deep that it burned the tar out of the seams and the color out of the wood. Ice-burn. The thing that happens to a man's hand when he holds iron in the white country in the dark months. The skin goes white and then black and then sloughs away. I look at her serpent prow. And I look past her, at the haze to the north, at the soft white-grey that is not quite mist and not quite sky, and I see — I think I see — shapes in it. Low. Long. A great many. Too many to count. Standing in the haze the way the figure stands at the grey ship's prow, still, waiting, and I blink and the haze is haze.

*"The Children of the forest dance in halls of redwood long forgotten."*

I do not know that one. I see Jensen's face as he hears it, and I see him not know it either, and that is the worst thing I have seen on this deck since the grey ship came out of the haze. Jensen always knows. Jensen had a grandmother who knew everything and put it into him by the fire. And his face as he hears *the Children of the forest* is the face of a man who has gone to a chest where he keeps every precious thing he owns and found it empty.

*"The far north wakes again."*

The hand on the back of my neck tightens until it hurts.

*"What is dead may never die."*

Silence.

It does not end. It stops. The woven voice goes out of the air like a lamp blown out, and the regard lifts off the fleet the way a hand lifts off a table, all at once, and the absence of it is so total that I stagger. My knees go. I catch myself on the rail with one hand and you against my chest with the other, and I hear men behind me go down — I hear the thud of knees on planking, two, three, a dozen — and I hear across the water from the Járnkjaftr a man screaming, high and steady and wordless, the way men scream when they wake from a dream they cannot get out of.

And you gasp.

A huge ragged pull of air, like a woman breaking the surface after being held under, and your whole body jerks in my arms and the white goes out of your eyes and the amber comes back, wild, staring, and you are shaking. Head to foot. Violently, the way a body shakes when it has been very cold for a very long time and is suddenly warm. Your hands come up and clutch at my forearms where they cross your chest and your nails go in. And there is a thump of wings and a weight on my shoulder, talons going into the same holes they have made over three days, and Skuggi is back, breathing hard, his beak open, his feathers ruffled out all over his body so that he looks twice his size, and he does not clack and he does not chitter. He presses his head against the side of my neck and is silent.

The men are looking at you.

I turn my head and see them. Every face on the deck. They are white. Torvald is on his knees with his hands flat on the planking. Ásgeir is still at the bow but he has both arms around the stem-post now and his forehead pressed to the wood. Ketill is standing, and his mouth is moving, and no sound is coming out. Sven is crying, silently, his young face wet. And they are looking at you, every one, with a thing in their faces that is not fear and is not awe and is both. The look men give the stories when the stories stand up and walk. But worse. Because the story that stood up and walked on the beach was a story about a god who gave. And the story that just went through them was a story about a world that is ending.

You do not look at them. You pay them no mind at all.

I feel you turn in my arms. Toward me. Your hands come off my forearms and go to my chest and fist in my tunic, and you look up at me, and your eyes are your own again — honey-amber, wild, but yours — and there is something in them that I have not seen there before. Not on the beach. Not in the mud. Not in the furs.

Fear. True fear. The fear of a woman who has seen a thing and cannot unsee it.

And you lean into me. Closer. Your body pressed to mine, seeking. My warmth. My touch. I feel you seek it. I feel you press your face for an instant into my chest the way you pressed it into my throat on the beach and breathe me in, and something in me roars up at the sight of the fear in your face, a thing so hot and so violent that it nearly takes me off my feet. *What did you see. What did it show you. Give me its throat.*

"Let me call the mist." Your voice is shaking. Not much. Enough. "I can cover the fleet. Make decoys of shadow they will follow. The sea will answer me — she will keep us off the rocks." Your hands tighten in my tunic. "But we cannot take the normal route." You turn your head. Look out over the rail, past the grey ship, to the horizon, and I watch you go still, and I watch something in your face listen, the way it listened on the beach before the raven came. "We must go west, Loki." Back to me. Your eyes on mine. "With haste."

"Impossible."

Jensen. Not to you. To the sea. His voice has gone to a breath. He is standing at the rail where he has stood since the grey ship came out of the haze, with his eyes on her, and his hand is not on his hammer. It is hanging at his side, open, and his face has gone the color of the haze, and he is staring at the grey ship's prow, at the forward platform, at the figure in the sealskin standing rooted by the serpents' heads.

And beside the figure, a wolf.

It is there. I did not see it come. It was not there and then it was there, the way Galdur came out of the smoke at Merewic, and I look at it and my mind will not take it in. It is the size of Galdur. Exactly. The same great shoulders that would come to your ribs, the same long legs, the same massive wedge of a head. It is grey. It was grey. Under the frost. Because it is covered in frost — thick, layered, white, like the rime on the altar after the pillar went out, furred over every inch of its pelt so that it looks carved out of hoar-ice, and the fur beneath the frost is not grey. It is dark. Clotted. Stiff. Soaked through with something that dried a long time ago and froze where it dried. Blood. Old blood, black-brown under the white, matting the fur of the chest and the forelegs and the belly in great frozen ropes, and the blood came from a wound. I can see the wound. In the chest. Low, behind the left foreleg, where a hunter puts a spear when he wants the heart. A wound that killed it. A wound no animal walks away from. And something is in the wound. Something dark and straight and broken off a hand's length from the hide, the snapped end of it jagged, frosted over like everything else.

"There is something in it." Kaleb. His voice comes from very far away. "In the chest. A spear-shaft. Snapped."

My father's first spear.

I know it the way I knew the wolf last night. Not from seeing — I cannot see a maker's mark at a bowshot, no man could. From the story. The one every boy in the fjord hears and is told never to ask about. Ott the Younger, the new Jarl, nineteen winters old with the Seat still warm under him, went over the Kveldfell into the white country in the dark of winter with thirty men, for a pelt no Jarl in the north could match, and he took his spear. His first spear. The one his father gave him when he was twelve, ash-shafted, the head pattern-welded with a band of gold wire at the socket, the one the skalds put a name to in the old lays. And he came back over the Kveldfell with nineteen men and without the spear. And when anyone asked where it was, he did not answer. And no one has seen it since.

Thirty winters. More. Before I was born. And the thing standing on the grey ship's prow beside the sealskin figure has a snapped spear-shaft in its heart and frost in its blood-soaked fur, and its eyes.

Its eyes are blue.

Not Galdur's blue. Not glacier. They are filmed. Clouded over with the milky skin that grows across the eyes of a thing that has been dead a long time, the eyes of a fish on a slab, of a drowned man pulled up after a week, dead eyes, eyes that should not see. And they are lit from behind the film with a blue that is not a color I know. A cold glowing blue, like the pillar, but wrong — not clean, not white-hot at the center. *Dim*. Steady. Unblinking. The blue of foxfire in a marsh, of the light that is sometimes seen on the masts of ships in a storm, that the old men say is the dead looking for the living. And they are not looking at the fleet. They are not looking at me.

They are looking at Galdur.

And Galdur is standing. At our knees, between the rail and us, risen to his full height, and he is bigger than I have ever seen him, every hair on his body standing up so that he seems to fill the stern. His lips have peeled back from his teeth. All the way. Back to the gums, back to the molars, so that every tooth in his head is bared to the root, and the sound that comes out of him is not a growl. I do not know what it is. It is lower than the sound he made at me on the beach, the refusal, lower than anything I have ever heard come out of a living throat, and it does not stop. It goes on and on, a vibration that I feel through the planking and through my boots and into my bones. Fury. Absolute, total, bottomless fury, and under the fury hate, a hate older than I have words for. And he has put himself — I see it, I see him do it — between the grey ship and *both* of us. Not you. Not me. Both. He has stepped to the rail and planted himself with his body across our legs and his great head low and his dead kin's glowing eyes on him, and he is not looking away.

Skuggi screams.

Off my shoulder. Once. The horn-cry. The stop-what-you-are-doing cry, but it is not aimed at the sky this time. It is aimed across the water. At the grey ship. At the dead thing on her prow. It is a challenge and a warning both, and it goes out across the sea like a thrown spear, and I feel the men behind me flinch from it.

For one moment the whole of the world holds still.

The sea is flat. I notice it now. I do not know when it happened. The swell has gone out of it, and the chop, and the faint ripple of the breeze, and the water between us and the grey ship is as smooth as oil, as smooth as the surface of a well, grey and still and reflecting the white sky so perfectly that the grey ship floats on it upside down, her serpents and her full sail and her still crew doubled, and there is no sound. No slap of water on our strakes. No creak of rigging. The Úlfsvaka is not moving. Not rocking. Every man on her is standing where he stands and no one is breathing, and across the water the Skarfr is still, and the Járnkjaftr is still, and the man who was screaming on her has stopped, and the whole fleet of the Wolf of the Fjord lies on a sea gone dead as glass with the haze to the north, and a dead ship off our quarter, and a dead wolf on her prow, and nothing in the world is moving at all.

And then the dead wolf lifts its head and sings.

The sound tears the air open. I do not have another way to say it. It is not a wolf's howl the way the howl at Merewic was a wolf's howl made larger. It is the thing a wolf's howl is a memory of. It starts low, so low that it is felt in the hollow of the chest before it is heard, and climbs, and it does not break at the top where a wolf's voice breaks into a yelp. It goes through the top. Into a long held note so high and so pure and so *cold* that I feel it in my teeth, in the roots of them, a frost forming in the nerves. And it goes on past where a wolf's breath would end. Past where a man's would. It goes on and on and on and it does not waver and it does not break, and I feel the hair on my arms try to stand and freeze, and my breath — my breath comes out of my mouth in a white cloud in front of my face. At midday. In autumn. In the thin white sun. My breath fogs in the air like a winter morning, and I look down and Kaleb's is fogging, and Jensen's, and yours, small white puffs coming from your parted lips as you stare at the dead thing singing on the grey ship's prow.

And it is not one voice.

Under it. I hear it as the note climbs, the way you hear the second drum under the first when the war-drums start. Others. Not from the grey ship. From the haze. From the north, from the soft white-grey that is not mist and not sky, where I saw the long low shapes and blinked them away — voices. Rising. A chorus of them coming up to meet the dead wolf's song the way the drowned come up to meet a lantern dropped over the side. Low and vast and wordless, a sound so huge that it does not seem to come from throats, as though the sea itself and the ice under the sea and the whole cold dark roof of the world were singing. And I understand — the way I understood the price on the beach, all at once and completely and with no way back from the understanding — that the world did not forget how to make this sound. The world forgot how to *hear* it. And it has been going on, in the cold under the cold, in the white country past the Kveldfell where my father lost eleven men and a spear, in the north where my father lost a brother and a hundred and thirty, the whole time. My whole life. It has been singing my whole life, and I never heard it.

Mildrith screams.

Under the forward thwart. A thin high Saxon shriek, like a rabbit in a snare, and then the Latin comes. Fast. Broken. Tumbling. *Libera nos a malo. Libera nos. Libera nos a malo.* Over and over in a voice gone up into a child's register, and no one turns. Not one man on the deck turns his head to look at her. I do not. I hear it and I do not turn, because there is a dead wolf singing on a dead ship and a chorus rising out of the north to answer it, and a Saxon girl's prayer to a god who never came to Merewic is the smallest sound on the sea.

"Silence her."

It is Hallr. At the steering oar. His voice has gone to an old man's croak and his straightened hands are white on the loom and he is not looking at Mildrith either. He is looking north. "Silence her. Gods' sake. The dead come to a voice that calls. *Any* voice. My grandmother said it in the Lofoten and her grandmother before. Do not call out on dead water. Do not call out to anything, or something will answer that you did not call."

Kaleb moves.

He goes forward along the deck fast and silent with his breath fogging in front of him, and he drops to his knee by the forward thwart, and his big hand goes over Mildrith's mouth. Over the Latin. Pressing it back into her throat. And she bites him. I see her do it. I see her teeth go into the meat of his palm below the little finger and I see her bite down until the blood comes, bright, running out from between her lips down her chin, and her blue eyes are wild and white-ringed and furious above his hand. And Kaleb does not take his hand away. He does not strike her. He kneels there with his palm bleeding into her mouth and his other hand on the back of her head, holding her still, and he puts his face close to hers and says something in a low voice, in Norse, too low for me to hear. And whatever it is, her eyes change. The fury goes out of them. And something else comes in, and her teeth loosen, and Kaleb does not take his hand away, and I mark it, and I put it with all the rest.

The song goes on.

I look down at you in my arms. Your face is turned up to the grey ship and the dead wolf and your breath is fogging and your eyes are wide, and the shadow-marks at the nape of your neck under the braid are burning black through the skin. And I find that I am not afraid.

I want to be plain. I was afraid last night with your empty body in my arms. I was afraid on the beach with you hanging in the fire. I am not afraid now. The thing that is in me now is the white. All of it. All of the white that did not get Thorolf's throat yesterday and all of the white that has been in me since I was seven, coming up from the base of my spine through every bone of my back like a man climbing a ladder, and it does not stop at the top and go quiet the way it always does. It keeps climbing. It keeps climbing because there is no throat. There is a dead wolf with my father's spear in its heart and a dead ship with my father's brother's name on her and a chorus of the dead singing out of the north, and I do not know what a blade does to them. I do not know if a blade does anything. I am standing on the deck of my own ship with two seaxes on my hips and eleven hundred men at my back and I do not know if any of it means anything at all to what is singing on that water. And the not-knowing is the worst thing I have ever felt. Worse than the kennel. It is the kennel. It is being seven and alone in the cold with a thing that has a shape you know and nobody in it.

And you are in my arms, and you are *not* empty, and you have just asked me for something.

*West. With haste.*

"Kaleb."

He comes back aft with his hand bleeding. He stops beside me. His breath is fogging and his face is white and he does not say anything clever, which is how I know.

"West."

"West is Hjaltland." The words come out of him flat and fast, the way they come out of him when he is reading water, and I have never once loved him more than I love him for not arguing. For telling me the water instead. "West is the Hjaltland race in the autumn gales with the tide running through it like a millrace, and past it the Orkneyjar, and past them nothing. Grey sea. Nothing but grey until Ísland, and we are not victualled for Ísland, and the knarrs are too slow and too heavy for that water with the loot in them." He looks at the haze to the north, at the shapes that are not there. "But nobody follows a fleet into the Hjaltland race in autumn who wants to live. Not men. Not ships." His jaw sets. "And if she can keep us off the rocks, brother, we will come out the far side of it a week from home and a hundred leagues west of where anything is waiting for us, and we can come down on the fjord from the sea-side where no one watches." A breath. "If."

"Hallr." I do not turn my head.

The old man's voice from the oar. "West." No hesitation. "My grandson is on this ship." That is all he says. It is enough.

"Jensen."

He does not answer at first. He is still staring at the dead wolf. And when he turns his head to me his face is the face from the beach when he said *Hroptr*, white and wet and stripped. "Rán did not have them," he says. Very quietly. "Halldis said Rán had them. And Rán did not have them, Loki. Something else did." His hand finds the hammer. "Go west. Go west and do not look back, and do not let that thing see where we go."

"Horn," I say. And then louder, my voice going out over the deck and the dead flat water, the shield-wall voice, so that every man on the Úlfsvaka and the Skarfr beside us hears it. "*HORN*. One long and four short. Break station and follow the flagship, every hull, close as they can hold. Any captain who loses sight of my stern in the mist keeps his bow pointed at the sunset and his mouth *shut*. No horns after this one. No hails. No man on any deck calls out, for anything, until I give the word."

Brandr has the horn to his lips before I finish. The long note goes out over the water, and it sounds wrong — flat and small and dead in the cold air, swallowed by the song — and then the four short ones. And across the water I see the Skarfr's men moving, and Thorolf on his steering-platform with his arm raised in answer, and past him the Járnkjaftr and Bersi on his knees no longer, and past him the Gjallar, and past her the knarrs, and the oars coming down into the flat water all along the line.

And the grey ship's oars stop.

All at once. Every oar on the *Hrímfaxi*, every one of them that has been rising and falling to its own drum, stops at the top of the stroke and hangs there in the air, dripping. And then comes down. Together. In perfect time. Every blade biting the water at once, in one stroke, with one sound, a single flat *slap* that comes across the dead sea to us like a hand striking a table, and the grey ship's bow comes round.

Toward us.

The song does not stop.

"Wolf." Ketill. His voice has gone strange. "Wolf. The rail."

I look. The forward rail of the Úlfsvaka, at the bow, where Ásgeir is still clinging to the stem-post, has gone white. Frost. Coming up over the gunwale from the outside, from the strakes below where the hull meets the dead water, creeping up over the top of the oak and spreading inward along the rail toward the stern the way the frost spread across the clay on the beach. Fast. Faster than frost forms. And it is not even. It is not the smooth white fur of rime on a cold morning. It has a shape. It comes over the gunwale in patches, white on the dark oak, and each patch is the same shape, and the shape is a hand. Spread fingers. Four and a thumb. As though something in the water below had reached up and put its palm on the side of my ship and held it there, and then another, and then another, a row of white handprints climbing over the rail from the dead sea and walking aft toward the stern one after another, toward the oar where Hallr stands, toward the furs, toward us.

Ásgeir lets go of the stem-post and backs away from it with his hands up and his mouth open and no sound coming out, because I told every man on the fleet not to make one.

I look down at you.

You are looking at the handprints. And then you look up at me, and your breath fogs between us, white, and your eyes are honey and afraid and *yours*, and I take your face in my hand. The one that is not around you. I take your jaw the way I took it in the mud, the way I took it on the beach, the way it fits, and I bring your face up to mine, and I can feel the cold coming off the dead sea on my back and the heat coming off your spine on my chest, and the shadow-marks are burning so hard now that I can see them through the pelt.

"Call it, litla hrafn." My voice is low and rough and steady, and I do not know where the steadiness comes from. "Call your mist. Hide my ships. Take us west." My thumb moves along your cheek. "I have the body. I have you. Whatever you have to spend, spend it. And then come back to me."

The frost reaches the steering oar. Hallr does not take his hands off the loom. The white hand closes over the wood beside his straightened fingers, and he looks at it, and he looks at you, and he holds.

The song goes on. The grey ship comes on. And at her prow the figure in the sealskin lifts its head for the first time, and the hood turns, slowly, across the flat dead water. Past the Skarfr. Past Thorolf. Past me. To the white-haired woman in the Wolf's arms.

And stops there.

## The White Ones

### Hrafna

My hands raise. And something inside me rumbles in the absolute satisfaction of a power that has been waiting to be *unleashed* for twenty years of life.

The sea answers in a ripple of wave and current and mist that becomes *fog* — dense, blinding fog. The ship rocks and suddenly the current is pushing; meeting oar-stroke for stroke, steering as much as guiding. The men in the fleet behind us do not shout in alarm. Twenty-three ships, roughly eleven hundred men in total, including the four hundred shield-sworn warriors, and they follow the *Úlfsvaka* with absolute trust that tells me more of the commander you are than any story in a hall could. I hear the pitches made, lines going out, ship to ship, silent in the water that churns beneath us, to keep them with us through the fog without horn and exposure needed.

---

I focus and feed the seiðr like I never have—pouring my heartbeat into the mist, my breath into the white, until my vision swims at the edges and the runes along my ribs burn white-hot. The fog responds, thickening, twisting, becoming walls and corridors and false horizons that lead nowhere and everywhere at once. It is not merely concealment. It is *confusion*, a labyrinth of wet air and whispered doubt that will send even the most seasoned navigator in circles until his oarsmen weep for sight of stars.

Shadow ships, designed to match us, take shape on the rolling waves. Decoys to feed the illusion. Voices no man makes call out orders from the shadow fleet, mimicking that of command from human throats.

Shadow voices from shadow men on shadow ships.

At one point, my right hand finds yours. I do not pull away when you pull me firm against you, back to your chest, chin resting on the crown of my head. The contact grounds me; wolf-warmth of Galdur on one side, warrior-strength at my back, Galdur's steady breathing and your rough palm on my belly anchoring me to the deck as my spirit strains toward dissolution. This is the cost of my *seiðr*—especially this kind. It is nature, yes. But it is also shadow and war — the Allfather's presence is all around us in this, but there is another every one of the men feels at once as well.

It comes with the dark roll of the clouds above, that aid the fog even more, and it sits heavy on the deck like static in the air around us. It is Kaleb — who has come back to join Jensen, you and I — who goes still and names it in a voice I have not yet heard him use. "Is that — Jensen is that— " He looks to Jensen and there is something in his eyes you have never seen, and I feel you see it. "Þórr. That is *Þórr*."

The storm arrives without announcement. Just as the one in the village did. Where the sky was grey-lit and fog-hidden, it changes without warning. Clouds of heavy black come on air that has suddenly gone silent and dense with a presence every man feels in his chest.

"Herföðr. And Þórr." Jensen breathes in awe. He looks at you with wet eyes he will not hide or be shamed for. "This is *their* domain. Their hands in this." 

Lightning lashes the sky as if in response to his words. Thunder rolls in the distance, bold as any war-hall drum. The wind bears down, aiding the sea and my seiðr and every pull of the men's oars. The decks are all silent and not just from the threat of the dead ships in pursuit.

---

The more hours we sail, the more the fog drinks my warmth, my awareness, and my edges. I am becoming the mist, losing definition, forgetting where Hrafna ends and the shadow world begins.

Galdur leans into me and then I feel it — your lips at my ear, voice low, rumbling with a warrior's growl and a man's need, and *anchoring*. You run your lips down the column of my throat, tracing skin and rune with teeth, grounding me back to earth and flesh. Your hand, heavy and warrior-strong, runs a pattern over my stomach as if memorizing the curves no dress nor cloak could fully conceal. There is an intimacy in it I am learning is *ours*. It turns the blood in my veins molten as sensation mixes with seiðr and the rhythm of the deck under us. For a warrior who would swear he knows nothing of comfort or soothing, you do it for me with the expertise of a man experienced in such things.

---

"Reef," I whisper sometime later, exhausted but refusing to quit, and the word feels distant, as though spoken by someone wearing my skin, yet I know it is me. "Starboard... low. Stone teeth. They hunger for hulls."

Kaleb's voice comes to us grim and tense. "We have reached the Hvítingar brother." There is a collective pause, where every man alive on this deck goes utterly still at his words and what they know follows them. The White Ones — a nine-mile field of drying skerries that has taken more hulls than any war in the north, and no captain alive takes a loaded fleet inside them in autumn.

I do not know how I know. The fog speaks to me in fragments, showing me shapes beneath the surface—jagged silhouettes, ancient and patient, waiting to tear open bellies and spill men into the cold. My eyes are closed now. I see through the mist itself, through the droplets that bead on your beard, on Galdur's fur, on the salt-stiffened wool of the crew.

The crew's fear is thick in the air. I can taste it same as the salt-spray and hunger in my gut. My nails dig into the flesh of the arm you have still banded around me, and I reach for everything I have inside me. "Trust me." I whisper to you, knowing what it is I ask and the cost it lays at my feet if I fail.

I *push*, hands shaking with the strain of intent, reaching for the water, urging the current to not obey, but assist. To guide and *protect*.

The sea and the gods riding it, answer. The impossible becomes possible once more.

The crack of flesh-on-flesh sounds across the deck sometime after we pass the reef and its dangers, and with it comes a scrape of steel and a woman's scream.

I raise my head in time to see Geirmund — a massive, dark eyed warrior with a scar over his left eye that could not see before the beach and now can, who I have not spoken to — directly, standing over a bloody faced Mildrith. At first glance, it would seem he simply struck her. Until I see the bright red of blood coming from his chest where she managed to stab him with something. The warrior does not even take true notice of it; he clearly has had worse done to him and cared even less then of it. A cracked flask of water lays forgotten and spilled at his feet; water he had tried to give her out of recognizing her thirst. She stabbed him for it.

Geirmund is forty-four winters, who lost a wife and two daughters four winters past. For all his size and bulk, he is still a father. He is a father and a warrior and most of all of it, he is a *good man*, with a heart of pure gold to match his size.

The look in Mildrith's salt-stung blue eyes is wild with contempt and the fury of entitlement not yet broken out of her. "Do not touch me, northern *dog*." She spits at Geirmund's feet. "What did I say that is not truth?!" 

Her voice shrieks for the whole of the deck to hear. "You all follow the witch blind like *fools*. She is *nothing*! She should have been strangled in the snow where she was found. Parlor tricks have you stupid dogs worshipping a *whore*—"

I barely catch myself as I find myself dragged with you moving directly towards her with a violence I have not seen out of you yet. That is not what makes the heart in my chest beat differently. It is that the violence is not because she managed to stab a warrior who does not truly care for the hole. It is because you watched my face change at her words, because exhaustion has stripped my defenses.

I can feel the tension in you. Later, when I rest between your legs on the furs, I can feel it in every part of you against me. Perhaps I am simply raw — every channel blown wide in the effort of the hours spent. But I can *feel* what it is that you chew on the most. It is weighing on your mind, etched into the place in the wolf-heart you can never fully block. It is the cost of a son who is bastard-born, yet still a son. I do not reach for it. "You are not wrong to not feel." You look down at me, surprise on every part of your stress-lined face. My hand comes to cup your jaw and my voice scrapes but holds. "The black and the dragons fighting — you did not flinch at the thought of killing your brother and that is eating at you now." You go completely and utterly still. My eyes, weary amber that is dimming but still present, find yours. "The ambush was designed by no mortal man, and you are not wrong for being the son on the right Loki. He was born and handed the left. You fought and became the right."  The green of your eyes flares bright, and I do not flinch from it. I speak the truth, because there will be nothing but truth between us for as long as I draw my breath, even if it earns me the back of your hand.

"Your father did not send you to the village either. I am not all-knowing, but there are things I do know. This is one of them. He did not send you here to die." My voice softens to the tone that is fast becoming yours, the way my hands on your shoulders and my body under yours in the furs is.

I have never once cared if another being was hurting the way I care that you are.

A strange horn cuts through the mist—far away now. The haunting sound is like no hunters horn I have ever heard. It carries beneath it something else—rhythm without melody, voice without breath. The sealskin ship, I realize. They are calling through the fog, speaking a language that predates oar and hull.

They are turning away, hunting the illusions of our ships rather than us. We are safe for now.

I slump against you where we have come back to stand at the stern, utterly exhausted. The fog I called begins to thin without my will to sustain it, peeling back in ragged strips to reveal grey sky and black water and the distant promise of cliffs we cannot yet name. My legs buckle. Your arm catches my waist, hauling me upright, and I feel the protest of my body—the drained runes along my ribs now cold and hollow, the fever-heat replaced by something deeper, more dangerous. The price exacted, the shadow runes *finally* fed for the first time in twenty years of life.

"We are safe. They bought the illusion of the fleet and followed." My voice rasps to you. My head pounds with the cost of Sight and power. 

When I turn, the crew is silent, staring at us. I expect still, with the habit of a woman who has all her life been trained to see judgment and fear, to see it on the faces there. That part of me expects steel drawn to cast down the *seiðkona*. Instead, I see something that stops my breath. It is not hate; it is understanding and acceptance. *Awe*. The word sits heavy, foreign—like a gift I did not ask for and do not yet fully trust. 

I turn my face into your throat, hiding the wetness that finally spills from my eyes. The exhaustion makes me soft, and I do not know how to handle being soft like this. Skuggi shifts on your shoulder, wings rustling, and I feel his gaze like a weight—ancient, loving, and *proud*. Galdur pushes his head against me until I drop one hand to his head, and he refuses to let me pull it back.

"We need shelter," I whisper against your skin, tasting salt and smoke and the iron of your possession. "Land. Soon. Long enough to let the ships give in to the loss of their prey. The sea speaks of water that is not safe yet and I need—" I tuck closer to you, words struggling to come. "Loki, I need earth. The wilds, away from village and threat. I need to regain my power if I am to keep us safe."

"Skógey."

Before you can respond, Jensen speaks. His face still carries the ash-pale remnant of what this day has showed him and hours of sailing in the truth of his faith made concrete, but his voice is steady, his dark eyes fixed on you with the focused clarity of a man who has been running calculations and landed on an answer that he knows will land hard. My rune-cold hands tighten once against your chest. "What is Skógey?" I ask, looking between Jensen and you and Kaleb, reading the tension radiating from you.

Every northern son who has sailed these seas knows of Skógey and the stories that surround the twin islands. Stories of old-growth forest dense with more than bark and soil. The kind that has been growing since before Ott's grandfather's grandfather split his first keel-timber. A stone circle is whispered to be somewhere in the interior, older than any Norse settlement, carved with marks in no language ever recorded. 

It is also said the islands are... occupied by something that has not bothered with anyone in living memory. The oldest stories say it they are not of this world. that they belong to the *Old World*, same as the deep north.

Jensen is not wrong though, and I see it on your face written plain now that I know how to read your expressions. It is the closest land. In the end, it is not a choice made by you alone. The choice to go is made when I cough against you and your hand comes back from my mouth red and wet.

### Loki

Your hands leave mine and rise, and I feel what rises with them before I see a single breath of what it does. It comes up through your back into my chest. I have you locked against me, one arm across your belly and the other hand still at your jaw, your spine burning black through the pelt against my breastbone, and as your arms lift — slowly, the palms down at first and then turning, the written palms turning up to the white sky the way a woman turns her hands to learn whether it has begun to rain — something in you *rumbles*. I feel it in my own ribs. A long low shudder, deeper than Galdur's growl and older, the sound a frozen river makes in the spring when the ice has decided to go and has not yet gone, and threaded through it so plainly that I could not mistake it for anything else if I lived a thousand winters, satisfaction. I know that feeling. It lives at the top of the white, at the instant the throat is finally under the edge, the long dark loosening of a thing that has been chained and suddenly is not, and I have never once in my life felt it from the outside, from another body, against my own skin. *Twenty years*, it says, whatever it is. *Twenty years of waiting to be let off the leash, and now.* My arm closes across your belly. I do not tell it to. It closes the way it would close on a shield-brother who has taken a blow and may go down, and you do not go down, and the sea answers you.

It answers from underneath. The water between us and the grey ship has lain as flat as a polished blade since the dead wolf began to sing, so still that the *Hrímfaxi* floats on it doubled, her serpents above and her serpents below, and now the whole of that dead skin shivers. There is no wind to do it. It shivers the way the hide of a horse shivers to throw off a fly, all at once, from one edge of the world to the other, and the doubled ship breaks into pieces on it and does not come back together. And then the sea breathes out. I can call it nothing else. The mist comes up off the water everywhere at once, not rolling in from the north the way sea-fog rolls, not settling from the sky, but rising from the surface itself in a white sheet as though the sea had been holding its breath for the length of a dead wolf's song and had finally let it go — thin at first, a haze over the swells like the steam off a horse in winter, and then thicker, and then thick, climbing past the oar-ports and the gunwale and the shields, and I watch the forward half of my ship go out like a lamp. The wolf-prow first. Ásgeir with his hands still raised. The mast to the yard, and then the yard, and the sail above it a grey ghost and then nothing, and the fog comes aft along the deck toward us over the bowed heads of my rowers so fast that I see Torvald's face for one breath with his mouth open and then I do not see Torvald at all. It reaches the stern and goes over us, cold and wet and so dense that when I look down at you in my arms I can see the white of your hair and the black of the pelt and the burning knot of the marks at your nape, and past the reach of my own arm I can see nothing.

It does not smell like sea-fog. That is the first thing I know about it, and I know it with my body before my mind has caught up. Sea-fog stinks of salt and weed-rot and the cold flat iron of deep water. This smells of wet stone after rain. Of earth turned over by a plough, of cut grass, and far under that, faint, the before-snow sharpness that has been on your skin since the god burned the flint off it, and I understand that I am breathing you. That the white closing over my ship and my men and my fleet is a thing that came out of you, and that I am taking it into my chest with every breath, and the thought goes through me hot and strange, and I hold you tighter, and I breathe it deeper.

At the steering oar Hallr makes a sound. It is not loud — he is the one who told us not to call out on dead water and he will die before he breaks his own law — only the small hard catch of an old man's breath, and I turn my head and I can just see him, a grey shape at the loom a stride and a half from me with his straightened hands on the wood, and I can see the frost. The white hand that closed over the steering oar beside his fingers as the hood turned is still there, and as the fog comes over it, it begins to *run*. The way rime runs when you breathe on a frozen blade — the edges first, going soft and wet, the white going grey and then clear, and then the fingers of it sliding. Down the loom. Slowly. It does not melt the way frost melts, from the outside in, in drops; it slides, all of a piece, the whole spread-fingered shape of it drawing back down the oar toward the water the way a hand draws back from a thing it wanted and has been told it may not have, reluctant, the fingertips last. Hallr watches it go. He does not take his hands off the loom. When it is gone he looks at the wet place where it was and then at you, a long look through the fog with his old face gone to wood, and then he looks back at the sea that he cannot see, and his lips move, and I do not hear what he says to it.

Then the ship moves under us, and Hallr nearly loses the oar. The *Úlfsvaka* has lain dead in the water since the sea went to glass; forty oars came down when I gave the order and I felt them bite and felt the hull answer, sluggish, the way a ship answers in a flat calm with a cargo of loot in her belly. Now she *lifts*. I feel it in the soles of my feet before anything else, a long smooth rise through the keel as though something broad and strong had come up underneath her and put its shoulder to her bottom, and then she surges. Forward. West. The oars bite and the water does not lie under the blades and let itself be pulled the way water does; it *pushes back* to meet them, stroke for stroke, every pull doubled, so that the men on the benches feel it in their arms and I hear a gasp go down the deck in the fog, men nearly going backward off their benches because they pulled with their full weight against a sea that was already pulling with them. And the steering oar goes live in Hallr's hands. I see him feel it. The loom jerks in his grip like a thing with a mind of its own and his whole body braces to hold it, and then he goes still, because it is not fighting him. It is *steering*. Leaning. Showing him. His hands go loose on the wood, as loose as I have ever seen them, the way a man's hands go loose on the reins of a horse that knows the road home in the dark better than he does.

"Wolf." The barest breath. His eyes are wide in the fog. "She has the ship." He swallows. "Gods. She has the *ship*."

I know. I can feel her have it. You are lying back against me with your arms raised and your eyes closed and your breath coming slow and even, and through your back I can feel the sea moving the way I feel my own blood moving — a vast cold pull beneath us, running west, and something in you riding it, *leaning* on it, the way you leaned on me at the rail. The current did not come because it was asked. It came because you reached down into it and took it by the scruff.

Behind us, in the fog, my fleet is out of sight. Every hull in it was strung out in sailing order a long bowshot apart, every captain with the horn's last note in his ears: follow the flagship close, and any man who loses my stern keeps his bow on the sunset and his mouth shut. And the fog has taken my stern. It has taken my stern from the man at my own steering oar. I listen and I hear oars — faint, muffled, the fog taking every sound and smothering it like a hand over a mouth, from no direction and every direction — and I cannot tell if they are my oars or theirs, and I feel the thing come up in me that comes up in a man who has held a line of shields together through a whole morning and feels, in the press, the wall beginning to come apart at the far end where he cannot see it.

Kaleb moves before I can speak. He is a shape and then he is a man beside me, with his bitten hand wrapped in a strip of his own tunic and the blood come through it black in the grey light, and he is holding the new halyard — Torvald's halyard, the one with the long eye-splice you drove the marlinspike through that morning — coiled over his shoulder in fat loops. He does not say anything. He puts his mouth by my ear and breathes one word, "Lofoten," and goes past me to the starboard quarter, where the *Skarfr* was the last time any of us could see her, and he sets his feet, and he looks into the white the way a man looks at a stretch of water he has fished all his life, and he throws. I watch the line go out into the fog, loop after loop paying out over the gunwale, the coil on his shoulder shrinking, and then the end leaving his hand and the whole length of it gone into the white and lying on the water somewhere none of us can see, and Kaleb stands at the rail with the bitter end wrapped twice round his good fist and does not breathe. Nobody breathes. The line lies slack for a long breath, and another, and another, long enough that I see his jaw begin to set, and then it comes up out of the water dripping and goes taut as a bowstring between his fist and the white, so hard that it hums, and he braces against it and grins — the boy's grin, the real one — and gives it three tugs. Short. Sharp. And waits. And out of the fog, along the dripping hemp, three tugs come back.

He turns his head to me with the grin still on him and his eyes very bright. "The cod-fleets," he breathes. "In the spring fog, off the Lofoten. My father's trick. Every boat on one rope, bow to stern, so that no man is lost and no man has to shout." He makes the line fast to the stern cleat with a hitch I could not tie with my eyes open. "Three pulls, all is well. Two pulls, slow. One long, stop. Many, trouble." He looks at the fog where the line goes. "Thorolf will pass it on. His mother was a Lofoten woman. He will tie his own line to his stern and throw it to Bersi, and Bersi —" he shrugs, a small one, because the fog makes every movement feel loud — "Bersi will work it out. He is slow. He is not stupid."

And I hear it happen. I hear my fleet become one thing. Somewhere behind us in the white, very faint, the soft wet slap of a coil hitting water. The creak of a line taking strain. The knock of an oar against a hull that is not ours, and then another, further off, and another further than that, going back and back into the fog along an invisible chain of hemp, hull to hull, from the *Skarfr* to the *Járnkjaftr* to the *Gjallar* and on down the line to the knarrs wallowing at the tail of it with their bellies full of Saxon silver, and through all of it, not one voice. Not a hail. Not a curse. Not one man on any deck calling out to ask what the line is or where it goes or what is happening. Eleven hundred men with a dead ship at their backs and a dead wolf singing in their ears and a fog on them that came up out of the sea at a woman's word, and they catch the line and tie it off and throw it on and lean into their oars in silence and follow my stern into the white, because I told them to follow it, and because I told them to be silent.

I have stood in front of my men on a hundred beaches and I have never once wondered what they were. I know what they are. They are the best fighting men in the north and they follow my prow because it brings them silver and glory and the fear of every coast from here to the Frankish rivers. And I am standing in the stern of my ship in a fog I cannot see through, holding a woman who is calling it, listening to eleven hundred men pass a rope down a line without a word because I said *mouth shut*, and the thing that closes in my throat has more weight in it than pride. A man can be proud of a good blade. This is the other thing — the thing a man feels when he leans on a blade in the dark to get up off the ground, and finds that it has held.

The song does not stop. It cannot be outrun, because the fog has taken its direction away. On the open water it came from the grey ship's prow, from one place, and I could put my back to it; now it comes from everywhere at once, from the white above and the white below, from the water under the keel and the wood under my feet, so that I feel it in the hull before I hear it, that long cold climbing note with the chorus under it, and the chorus is louder now, or nearer, or the fog has gathered it the way a cupped hand gathers water. It gets into the timbers. The *Úlfsvaka* hums with it the way a drinking horn hums when a man runs a wet finger round its rim. I feel it in my teeth. I feel it in the cold that comes off the sea, which the fog has not touched — your fog is earth-smelling and almost warm against my face, but under it, down at the waterline, the cold of the dead water is still there, and it is following.

And then the shapes come. You make them; I feel it in you when it starts. A pull, a spending, something going out of you through your raised hands the way blood goes out of a cut, your breath catching, and the marks at your ribs flaring under my arm so hot that the wool of my own tunic on you begins to smell scorched. And out in the fog, off the port beam, the white thickens. Darkens. Gathers itself into a long low shape that slides past us going the other way, north and east, back toward the haze where the dead came from, and I know her before I know that I know her.

She is the *Úlfsvaka*.

The wolf-prow. The striped sail, red and blue, with the patch Torvald put in it off Jutland. My shields along her gunwale, with the black wolf's head painted on the boss of the third one aft where Brandr painted it badly his first week aboard and I let it stand. Her oars rising and falling in good stroke. A man at her bow with his arms raised — Ásgeir. A man at her steering oar with a grey head bent over the loom — Hallr. And at her stern a tall man with an ash-blonde head and a black pelt on his shoulders, holding a white-haired woman against his chest with one arm across her belly, the two of them standing exactly as we are standing, and I look across the fog at myself, and the shadow-man at the stern of the shadow-ship does not turn his head. He looks straight ahead into the white, north-east, toward the dead, and his face is mine, and his face is made of smoke. The edges of him are not edges. They smoke away into the mist the way the edges of a coal smoke when you blow on it, and the white hair of the woman in his arms streams backward into nothing, and the whole ship is grey, every color I named a grey of a different weight, a thing drawn on the fog in ash. Behind her come others. The *Skarfr*, with a shadow-Thorolf on her steering-platform. The *Járnkjaftr*. The *Gjallar*. The fat shapes of the knarrs. Gliding past us one after another the wrong way, toward the song, and on our own deck I hear forty-six men see it — the small sounds men make when they look at a thing they have no hall-story for. Somebody's breath going out of him in a long hiss. Somebody's knees finding the planking. Torvald's voice, a whisper so thin it barely carries, beginning a charm against the night-hags his mother taught him and stopping when the man beside him puts a hand over his mouth. At the steering oar, Brandr has the horn in both fists against his chest with his knuckles white on the bone of it, and his face turned to the shadow of his grandfather passing at the shadow-oar, and his lips pressed together so hard they have gone bloodless, and he does not lift it. He does not lift it. I will remember that until I die.

And then the shadow-ships speak. "*HORN.*" Across the water, from the stern of the shadow-*Úlfsvaka*, from the shadow-man with my face, in a voice that goes out over the fog the way my voice goes out over a beach — and it is mine. It is mine to the bottom of it. The roughness in it from three nights without sleep. The Norse of the fjord with the Saxon under it that I have spent twenty years sanding out and never quite got clean. "*One long and four short. Break station and follow the flagship.*" And from further off, from the shadow-*Skarfr*, Thorolf's deep rolling voice calling his oars, and from further than that a voice I know better than my own, crying "*Brace round, you whoresons, brace her round, are you rowing or fucking your benches —*" and it is Kaleb's. Kaleb's to the life. The crack in it at the top. The laugh under the curse.

The real one beside me goes very still. "I do not sound like that," he breathes, barely sound at all. Offended. A man in a fog on a hunted ship with a dead wolf singing in the timbers, offended.

"You sound exactly like that," I breathe back.

He looks at me. I look at him. And something passes between us that is the oldest thing we have, older than the yards, the look of two boys who have just been caught in the same piece of mischief, and he presses his bitten fist against his mouth and his shoulders shake once, silently, and I feel my own mouth move, and for the space of a breath, in the fog, with the dead behind us, my brother and I are laughing at nothing without a sound, and it is the most frightened I have ever seen him.

The voices go on, north-east, fading, the whole ghost-fleet hailing itself in a dozen voices I know, and behind us, far behind, the song changes. The long note breaks into a lower one, and the chorus under it shifts the way a pack shifts when the lead wolf turns its head, and the whole vast cold sound of it begins, very slowly, to move toward the voices. Away from us. Toward the noise and the shapes and the ships made of you. *The dead come to a voice that calls*, Hallr said. His grandmother in the Lofoten. *Any voice.* You made them a voice to come to. I feel you feel it work. The marks at your ribs pulse once against my forearm, hot as a forge-coal, and you let out a breath with a small low sound of effort at the bottom of it, and your head tips back against my collarbone, and the fog thickens round us.

The next voice is not one of yours. I know that before I know anything else about it. It comes from the starboard side, close, closer than the shadow-ships ever came, from the white a few oar-lengths off our quarter where there is nothing but fog and water, and it does not call an order. It sings. Low. A woman's voice, small and hoarse and tired, the voice of a woman singing at the end of a long day with her arms aching, very quietly, so that the dogs will not wake. In Saxon. I know the tune. I hummed it to you in the blue dusk with your hair in my hands. I have known it since before I knew my own name, and I have not heard it sung with words since I was seven years old, and the words come across the water to me now in the West Saxon of the chalk country, in her voice, in my mother's voice, *slǣp nū, lȳtling, se wulf is feorr* — sleep now, little one, the wolf is far — and the whole of me stops. My heart does a thing in my chest like a foot missing a stair in the dark. And my mouth opens. I feel it open on its own, the way your hands rose, and I feel the word come up into it from somewhere under everything, under the kennel and the yards and the white, the word I have not said aloud in twenty-one years, and the shape of it is already on my tongue, *mōdor* —

A hand clamps on my forearm. Jensen. I did not see him come. He is at my left with his fingers locked round my arm above the wrist so hard that I feel every bone of his hand, and he is not looking at me. He is looking out into the fog to starboard, toward the voice, and his face. I have known that face since we were six. I have seen it weep at the fire and go white at a wolf and close like a door at the spine-marks, and I have never once seen it like this. He heard it. He knows it, because he was six years old with stolen bread in his tunic crouched in a kennel doorway in the dark, listening to a Saxon thrall-woman sing her son to sleep among the dogs, and he has not heard it since either. His lips are pressed white. His head moves, once, side to side.

I close my mouth. It costs me more than not killing Thorolf and more than taking my hand away from you on the furs, and I do not know why, and I do not want to know. I close my mouth on the word and feel it go back down into me unsaid, and Jensen's hand stays on my arm, and the voice in the fog goes on singing, *se wulf is feorr, se wulf is feorr*, the next line, the one after that — and stops. Mid-line. On the word before the last word of the third verse. And the stopping goes into me like a blade into the joint of a knee, because that is where she stopped. Every night. That word. Because that was where the old bitch at the back of the kennel would lift her head and growl at the sound, and my mother would stop to let her settle, and most nights she fell asleep herself before she started again, and I would lie in the straw with my face against her arm and wait for the last word that never came. I have never told a living soul where she stopped. Not Kaleb. Not Jensen. Not Ylva. And whatever sang to me out of the fog stopped exactly there.

I am shaking. I find it out by looking down: my hand on your jaw is shaking against your skin, and my arm across your belly, and I cannot make them stop. You do not stir. You are far out in the white with your arms raised and your eyes closed, and I do not know if you heard it. I do not know if it was you — if some part of you reached into me the way the god reached into you and found the song I hummed into your hair and gave it a voice to throw at the dead, and got the stopping-place right, which you could not have known — or whether it came up out of the dead water to a man who still has a name the dead can find. I do not know. I want a throat. I would give my right hand for a throat.

Jensen's fingers loosen on my arm without letting go. He puts his mouth by my ear and breathes, so low it is more shape than sound, "That was not her, Loki."

That is when the hand comes over the stern. I see it because I am looking down, because I cannot look at the fog to starboard anymore and have looked at you instead, at your bare feet on the planking between my boots, the toes pale and the nails faintly blue with the cold that has come up through the deck, and past your heel, at the edge of the furs where the planks meet the sternpost — white. A white shape climbing over the stern from the water beneath, spreading on the dark oak. Four fingers and a thumb. Then the heel of the palm. As though a hand had come up out of the sea behind my ship and reached over the rail and laid itself flat on my deck, and then, as I watch, it *moves*. The frost blooms forward from it in a new print, a hand's length closer, fingers spread, and then another, closer still. Walking. Toward the furs. Toward your bare heel.

My seax is in my hand. I do not remember drawing it; it is the one thing in me that has never needed remembering. I am bent with you still against my chest and the nine inches of pattern-welded steel going down into the nearest print in a short hard stab, the way I would put it through the back of a man's hand to pin it to a table, point-first through the white into the wood. And the cold comes up the blade. It comes so fast that I see it — the frost racing up the steel from the point, the twisted bands of dark and bright going white one after another like grass going under snow, up to the guard, over the guard, onto the antler grip, into my hand. Into the bones of my fingers. I have held iron on the Kveldfell in the dark of the year and felt my palm freeze to it, and this is something else, a cold that *bites* the way fire bites, and it goes up my wrist and my fingers go dead on the grip and my arm goes dead to the elbow, and I cannot let go of the seax because my hand has frozen to it, and the print under the point does not care. It does not stop. The blade is through the middle of it pinning nothing, and the white blooms out round the steel and goes on, the next print forming past it, fingers reaching, a hand's length from your heel.

Steel does nothing.

I have known it since the dead wolf lifted its head. I have known it in my belly with the cold and I have not let myself know it in my mind, and now it is in my hand, frozen there, in the dead bones of it, and I know it everywhere. Twenty-eight years, and every answer I have ever had for anything is a blade, and I am kneeling on my own deck with my blade through a dead thing's handprint and it is not even slowing down. And Galdur puts his foot on it. He comes from your other side, from where he has stood since the grey ship came out of the haze with his body across both of us, and he does not hurry. He takes one step and sets one great forepaw down flat on the newest print, the one reaching for your heel, the whole spread weight of him coming down onto the white the way a wolf pins a hare, and he looks down at it, and the low sound comes out of him, the one from the rail, so deep I feel it in the deck more than hear it — and the frost under his paw *hisses*. A thin grey thread of steam curls up round the pads of his foot, and the white shape under him goes grey, goes wet, goes clear, the fingers of it drawing back, and then the print behind it, and the one behind that, the whole walking line of them going out like a row of candles in a wind, back across the planking to the stern rail and over it and gone, down into the water they came out of. Galdur lifts his foot. Where it stood there is a paw-print pressed into the wood, dark and wet and steaming, as if the oak had gone soft under it for a heartbeat. He looks at me with the glacier eyes. Old, vast, patient, nothing of you in them now, only the thing from the white country that looks at men the way men look at weather, and it looks at me kneeling there with my frozen useless blade through a print that is already gone, and I swear on every god listening that what is in its eyes is not contempt. It is something worse. It is pity. And he lies down across your feet.

The feeling comes back into my hand slowly, in needles, and I work my fingers off the antler grip one at a time, and the seax comes away from the deck with frost still feathered along its edge, and I look at it. The best blade I own. The one you cut your palms with. I sheathe it, and I do not draw it again for the rest of that day, and I have never in my life hated a sound the way I hate the sound it makes going home.

Your right hand comes down out of the air where it has been raised all this while, and it finds mine — the one I have just worked free of the frost, the fingers still half-dead and burning with the blood coming back into them. You do not open your eyes. You do not look. Your hand simply comes down out of the white the way a bird comes down to a known branch, and closes on mine, and I feel the cold in your fingers. It is not the frost's cold. It is your own, coming up out of you from somewhere deep, the warmth going out of your hands the way it went out of them the night you walked my deck in the wolf, and I take your hand and pull. You come. Your back against my chest, all of it, the full length of you settled into me from the crown of your head under my chin to your heels between my boots, and I put my chin down on the top of your head, on the white part where the braid begins, and lock my other arm across your belly with my palm spread flat on it, and Galdur leans his weight against your legs from the side, and between the three of us you are held. That is the word in me. *Held.* As though you might otherwise blow away. As though you are a sail that has been let go and is filling and filling and will tear free of the yard if no one keeps a hand on the sheet.

You lean into it, your whole weight, and your fingers tighten on mine, and through them, coming up out of you and into me, I feel the thing you are spending. It goes out of you the way heat goes out of a stone in the night, steady and complete, and the sea is in it and the earth-smell is in it, but under them, in the pull of it, there is a darker thing, a thing with teeth, and I feel it at the base of your spine against my belly where the shadow-marks burn, *feeding*, and I feel something else feel it too. The Allfather is here. He has been here since the vision; the hand on the back of my neck has not lifted, it lies there warm and heavy as it has lain for three days, and His regard is round us in the fog, in the white, in the way the current leans, lying over the fleet like a cloak laid over sleeping men. I am beginning to know that one the way I know the weight of my own pelt. And the thing that comes now is not Him.

It comes from above. The fog has been grey-white, lit from somewhere overhead by the thin sun we cannot see, and the light goes, as though a hand had been passed over the lamp. The fog round us goes from white to grey to the color of a bruise, and the air thickens. It goes heavy and close and *charged*, the way the air goes in a forge when the smith has been working a long time and the iron is white and you can feel the heat of it on your face from across the shed, and every hair on my arms stands up, and it is not from cold. It is a pressure. It comes down on the deck and into my chest like the moment before a blow lands, a held breath the size of the sky, and a sound goes down the benches in the dark fog, forty-six men drawing breath at once and not letting it out.

Kaleb has come back aft. He stands at my right with Jensen at my left, the two of them a pace off, close enough that I can see their faces in the bruise-colored dark, and Kaleb's face is turned up to the sky none of us can see, and it is doing a thing I have never once seen it do in twenty years. It is open. All of it. The grin is gone from it, the thin one and the real one; the dry thing is gone, the little lift at the corner of the mouth that means he is about to say something that will make you laugh or make you want to put him through a wall. There is nothing on it but the dark light and his eyes, wide and grey and gone wet at the rims, and when he speaks it is in a voice I have not heard out of him, not in the yards or the shield-wall or the night Ívarr died. A boy's voice.

"Is that —" He turns his head. Not to me. To Jensen. "Jensen, is that —"

He stops. His throat works. And I see it come up into his eyes from somewhere he keeps very deep, deeper than his want for the Saxon girl and deeper than his love for me, and I feel you see it too — your eyes closed against my chest, your body far out in the white, and through the hand in mine a flicker of your attention turning toward him the way a flame turns in a draught.

"Þórr," Kaleb breathes. "That is *Þórr*."

And the storm comes. It does not build. It does not roll in from the horizon with the wind ahead of it and the first fat drops spattering the deck the way storms come at sea. It *arrives*, the way the storm arrived over Merewic when you lit the stone, all at once, the sky above the fog going from bruise to black in a single breath, black so heavy and so low that I feel it pressing on the top of my skull. The pressure breaks. The wind comes — from the east, behind us, over our stern, a great hard shove of air that hits the sail I cannot see and fills it with a crack like a shield splitting and drives the *Úlfsvaka* forward through the white so that I stagger with you in my arms and the whole hull heels and the oars bite and the current under us leaps to meet the wind like a dog leaping at its master's whistle. And the fog does not blow away. My mind will not take that. A wind like that would tear any sea-fog to rags in the time it takes to curse, and this one does not tear; it goes *with* us, the whole white wall of it moving west at our speed with the wind at our backs, wrapped round us like a cloak that does not fly loose, as though the wind and the fog had agreed between them. And over us in the black, the thunder. It does not crack. It *rolls*, long and deep and slow, from one edge of the sky to the other, the way the great war-drums in my father's hall roll when the Jarl walks in at Jól, and I feel it in my breastbone and the deck and the keel, and I feel the fleet behind us on the rope feel it, a shudder going down the line from hull to hull. And Kaleb makes a sound beside me that I will never tell anyone he made, and his hand comes up — not to a hammer; he has never worn one, he has laughed at Jensen's for twenty years — to the hollow of his own throat where a hammer would hang, and presses there, flat, and stays.

"My father gave me to him," he says. He says it to the black, so low that only Jensen and I hear it, and perhaps the raven. "Off the Hálogaland skerries. I was nine. We were caught out in the autumn gales in the little four-oared boat, and the mast went and the sea was coming over the bow, and my father took me by the back of the neck and held me up over the side, into the wind, the way you hold up a pup to show a buyer, and he shouted into the storm that if the Thunderer brought the boat in, the boy was his." His jaw works. "And the wind dropped. Like that. And the sea lay down, and we rowed in to Hálogaland on a sea like a pond." He does not take his hand from his throat. "I have told that as a joke for nineteen years, brother. In every hall from here to Dublin. How my father bargained me to a god for a four-oared boat and got the better end of it." His eyes are turned up to the black, wet, and he does not wipe them. "I know that hand. I felt it on the back of my neck when I was nine years old, and I have spent nineteen years telling myself I did not."

Lightning answers him. It splits the black overhead, a white rope of it running from the sky down into the sea somewhere behind us, and for one breath the whole fog is lit from inside, every droplet of it blazing, so bright that I see the bones of my own hand through the skin where it holds yours. The thunder comes on top of it, a crack and a roll at once that I feel in my teeth, and by the light of it I see Kaleb's face, and it is the face of a boy of nine held over the side of a boat by the back of his neck, and the face of a man of twenty-eight who has just been told by the sky that the joke was never a joke.

Jensen is weeping. He is never ashamed of it; he wept on the beach in front of seventy men and did not wipe his face, and he does not wipe it now. He stands at my left with the lightning in his wet eyes and his hammer in his fist and he looks at you, in my arms, with your eyes closed, with the face of a man watching every song his grandmother ever sang him come walking up out of the sea. "Herföðr," he breathes. "And Þórr." His voice cracks on the name. "This is their domain. The sea and the storm. The Hanged One and the Thunderer." He turns his face up to the black, and the rain begins, cold fat drops coming down through the fog onto his upturned face and running with the tears. "This is their hands in it."

The lightning comes again, and this time it is behind us. Far behind, back along our wake, where the fog is thickest and the song is coming from, and it does not strike once. It strikes and strikes and strikes, white rope after white rope driving down into the sea in the same place, again and again, the way a smith drives his hammer down on the same spot of iron, and the thunder of it comes over us in a roar that does not stop, and under the roar, for one breath, the song *breaks*. I hear it. The long cold note that has not wavered since the dead wolf lifted its head cracks in the middle like ice cracking and goes up into something that is no song at all — a shriek, high and thin and enormous, the sound of a sheet of ice the size of a field splitting down the middle — and the chorus under it goes ragged, voices breaking off, and through the fog, far behind, by the light of the strikes, I see a glow. Grey, not white. A long low grey shape lit from beneath, with steam coming off it, great boiling clouds pouring up from the water round it into the black, and I do not know what I am seeing and I know exactly what I am seeing.

"He fights them." Jensen, his voice gone to nothing in the roar. "Þórr does not come for men. Þórr comes for *thurses*. For the frost-folk. For the rime." He grips my arm, the second time today. "Gróa said. She said the Thunderer's hammer and the rime are the oldest enemies in the nine worlds, older than gods and men, and that wherever the frost walks where it should not, he comes. She said it like a thing that had not happened in a very long time." He looks back into the fog, at the steam. "It is happening."

The strikes stop. The shriek goes down into a groan, and the groan into silence, and in the silence, very slowly, far off and further now, the song begins again. It is quieter. It is still there.

We sail for hours. I do not know how many; the fog has eaten the sun and the storm has eaten the sky, and the only measure I have is the wind at my back and the rain on my face and the slow change in you, and the change in you is the thing I watch, because it is the thing that frightens me. You are growing cold. That I expected, and I braced for it. But this is not the cold of the night you walked in the wolf, the house with the hearth banked and no one home. This is a house with its walls coming down. You are lighter in my arms, the way a thing gets lighter when it is soaked through and the water begins to leave it, and your edges are going. The rain runs off my face and my beard and my shoulders and it does not run off you. It beads on your skin, and then it stops beading. It *joins*. The drops on your arm where it lies over mine go flat and spread and run together, and the line where your skin ends and the wet begins goes soft, the way a line drawn in charcoal goes soft when you rub it with your thumb, and I look at the hand that holds mine and I can see the fog through the tips of your fingers. Only a little. A grey glow at the edges of the nails, as though you were a lamp of horn and someone had lit the fog behind you. Your hair is wet and it does not lie heavy on your back the way wet hair lies; it lifts, the loose strands of it drifting up off your shoulders into the white as though there were no weight in them at all, as though they were going *into* it, and the braid I made, the five strands and the red thread, is the only part of you that seems to remember which way the ground is.

You are becoming the mist.

I know it the way I knew the wolf. You are pouring yourself into it to make it and to hold it and to send the shadow-ships and to lean on the sea, and the more of you that goes out the less of you comes back, and somewhere past the middle of the afternoon, if it is afternoon, I look down at your face in the bruise-dark and I cannot tell where your cheek ends and the fog begins, and the thing that comes up in me is not the white. It is the kennel. It is being seven. And I do the only thing I have.

I put my mouth to your ear. Low, under the rain, under the oars, under the song, so low that the men a pace off cannot hear it and only you and the raven on my shoulder can, and I speak to you. I tell you where you are. "This is your ear," I say against it, and take the edge of it between my teeth, lightly, and feel it go solid under the bite. "Mine." I put my mouth below it, on the hinge of your jaw, the place my hand has held since the mud. "This is your jaw. I have held it since the first hour. It fits my hand." I go down. Slowly. The rain running off my lips onto your skin. "This is your throat." The omen-marks under my tongue, cold, and I open my mouth on them and drag my teeth down the length of the column and feel the skin under them stay skin. "And this —" The bite. My bite, the oval of my teeth blue-black above the bronze wolf of the clasp, and I put my mouth over the whole of it and hold it there and suck, hard, until I feel the blood come up under it, until I taste the heat of you rising to meet my mouth through the cold. "This is where I started. This is where you begin, litla hrafn. Here, under my teeth. Whatever you are pouring into the sea, you begin here, and you end where my hands are, and I have my hands on you, and I am not taking them off."

My right hand is on your belly, flat under the pelt over the wool of my own tunic, and I begin to move it. Not the way I touched you on the furs, hunting. I move it the way I would learn the shape of a blade in the dark — slow, pressing, the heel of the palm and then the fingers, across the soft give of your belly and up to the hard band of the war-runes under your breasts where they burn through the wool, and down again to the jut of the hip-bone and across, a long slow circle, and again, pressing the shape of you into my palm and my palm into the shape of you, so that if you forget where your edges are, my hand will remember for you. The curve of the belly. The dip of the waist. The swell of the hip under it. Shapes no dress or cloak could hide from a man who had his hand on them, and I am memorizing them here, in the fog, with the dead behind me and the gods over me and my men a pace away, and I do not care who sees, and I do not stop. "Your belly," I say against your throat. "Softer than the rest of you. It gives under my hand like good bread. I learned that on the furs." The circle. "Your ribs. The war-marks. Hot. Hard. The muscle under them harder than a woman's should be, like a man who has pulled an oar." The circle. "Your hip. Here." I press the heel of my hand into the hollow inside the bone, and I feel you, I *feel* you, a shudder going through you under my palm, and the line of your arm over mine goes sharp again, the charcoal edge coming back. "I know every inch of this, kvinne. I have had three days and three nights with nothing to do but learn it. You do not get to give it to the fog. It is not yours to give." My teeth on your throat again, harder, the edge of a bite. "It is mine."

You make a sound. It is so small that I would not have heard it if my mouth were not on your skin, and it is neither the growl from the beach nor the hum from the sea chest. It is lower than both, a sound with heat in it and something under the heat, like a woman in deep water hearing her name called from the shore. And your hand in mine, the one I could see the fog through, tightens, and the grey at the edges of the nails goes out, and the blood comes back into your skin under my mouth — I taste it come back, the heat rising through the cold the way it rose under my palm on your breastbone the night you walked in the wolf — and the loose strands of your hair come down out of the fog and settle wet and heavy on your shoulders, and your weight against me is a woman's weight again.

You are still out there. Your arms are still half-raised, your eyes still closed, the fog still pouring out of you. But there is a rope on you now, and I am the rope, and for as long as the fog holds, I do not stop. My mouth at your ear, at your throat, on the bite; my hand moving on your belly in the long slow circles; telling you where you are in a voice that would not carry to the mast. Telling you about your hands, the written palms, the healing-marks warm on my shoulders in the red dusk. About your feet, which are cold, and which I will warm on my own belly when we are under a roof. About your mouth, and what it did to me when it said my name. I tell you things I have never told a woman because I have never once had a woman I needed to keep from dissolving, and I find the words are there when I reach for them, low and rough and filthy and true, and the blood goes hot in me with every one of them, my cock thick against the small of your back through the wool, the heat of you and the cold of the sea and the iron of the storm all running together in me until I cannot tell the wanting from the holding, and I do not try. Kaleb is a pace away. Jensen is a pace away. Neither of them looks at us. Both of them stand with their backs half-turned and their bodies set between us and the deck like two shields in a wall, and I know they hear none of it and all of it, and I do not care.

It is Jensen who comes and crouches at my feet, somewhere in the long grey middle of it, with the rain running off his hair. He does not look at you. He looks up at me from the deck with his hammer in his fist and the drawn, emptied face of a man who has carried a heavy thing a long way and has just found somewhere to set it down, and he keeps his voice under the rain. "I have to tell you a thing." His eyes go to Ketill, a grey shape at the rowing-deck rail. "And then I have to not have told it."

I lift my mouth from your throat enough to speak. "Tell it."

"Gróa had a word." Slowly. The second-tally voice, careful. "She would not say it inside. Not ever. Not under a roof. She would take me up to the stone behind the house on the dark of the moon and sit me with my back against it and say it there, once, and make me say it back, and then make me swear never to say it on water." His jaw tightens. "We are on water. I am going to break it, and I will pay what it costs." He wets his lips. "*Hrímnár*."

The fog takes the word. I feel you not move against me. I feel the raven on my shoulder shift and go still.

"Rime-corpses," Jensen breathes. "The dead of the frost. They are not draugar. Draugar are greedy and stupid and they keep to their howes and sit on their gold and break the backs of men who come to rob them, and you can kill a draugr if you are strong enough and know the trick — the head off, the head at the arse, the rest in the fire. Every boy knows that. Draugar are men who were too hungry to stay dead. The hrímnár are something else." He turns the hammer in his fist. "Gróa said that in the far north, past where the Sámi go, past where the reindeer go, past the edge of the white country, there is ice so old it remembers the time before the gods cut up Ymir and made the world out of him. Ice that was there when there was only Ginnungagap, and the fire to the south and the rime to the north, and the first frost-giant dripping into being in the place where they met. And she said that ice does not forget. And that sometimes, when men die in it — die cold, die far, die without fire and without a howe and without their names said over them — the ice keeps them. Not as draugar. As its own. It fills them with itself the way the sea fills a holed ship, and they go on. They do not want gold. They do not want anything a man would want." His eyes come up to mine. "She said they want warmth. All of it. Every warm thing. They want the world to be what it was before the fire came, and they go toward heat the way water goes downhill, and they do not stop."

The rain runs down my face. I do not say anything.

"Two things she said about them, over and over, until I could say them back in my sleep." He lifts a finger, and stops himself, and lets the hand fall, and I see him decide not to count. "They come to voices. Hallr's grandmother had that much. Any voice raised on cold water, they hear it. And the other —" He looks at Ketill again. Then at me. And his face is very white. "They come to names. She said if you speak a dead man's name on the water, a man who died in the cold, he hears it. Wherever he is. However far. He hears his name the way a boy hears his mother calling him in from the yard, and he turns his head, and he comes."

I see it land in him at the same moment it lands in me. I think of the deck a half-day gone, in the thin white sun, with the grey ship coming up out of the haze. Ketill at the rail with his hands shaking, saying *Steinn Ottsson had the Hrímfaxi*. Sven with his voice cracking, *Steinn —*. And Jensen. My flat, exact, faithful brother, who knows everything his grandmother ever put into him by the fire, standing at the rail with his eyes on the serpents and saying it clear and plain into the cold air over the water, *son of Ott the Elder*. And the grey ship's oars stopping all together, and coming down all together, and her bow coming round.

"It was turning before," I breathe. "It was coming before anyone said it."

"It was coming *along* us." He does not look away. "A bowshot off, running up our quarter toward the head of the fleet. Along. And then Ketill said his name. And I said it." His voice cracks and he lets it. "And it turned toward us, Loki. Toward the ship where its name was."

There is nothing on this sea to say to that. I put my hand on the back of his neck, the way the god's hand is on mine, and I hold it there, and he bows his head under it and does not move for a long time.

"There is a reason my father never says it," I say at last, low. "Steinn's name. Twelve years. *Ott never once spoke Steinn's name again.*"

Jensen lifts his head.

"And there is a reason," I say, "that a one-eyed woman stood up in the Hall twelve winters ago and told him the third ship's name was a calling."

We look at each other in the fog, and neither of us says Halldis, and neither of us needs to. Somewhere a long way behind us in the white the song goes on, a little quieter than it was, a little further off, and I wonder for the first time, with the rain running into my mouth, what else my father has never said aloud, and who taught him not to.

"Reef."

It comes out of you so softly that for a breath I think the fog said it. Your lips barely move. Your eyes are still closed, your head still back against my collarbone, and the word comes out of your mouth in your own voice but far away, as if you were speaking from the bottom of a well, or from the far side of a door. "Starboard." A breath. "Low." Another, and your fingers tighten in mine. "Stone teeth. They hunger for hulls."

I feel Kaleb go still before I see it, and then I see it — all of him stopping, the way a hound stops when it catches a scent it was bred to fear, his head coming round to the starboard bow and his face changing. He has been at my shoulder for hours with his bitten hand in his armpit and the line in his other fist, feeling the tugs come up it from the *Skarfr*, three and three and three, all well, all well, and he has not spoken since the thunder. He speaks now, grim and flat, in the voice he keeps for water. "We have reached the Hvítingar, brother."

And the whole deck stops. Through the rain and the oars and the hum of the song in the timbers I hear forty-six men stop — the oars go on, the men pulling them do not dare let them stop, but the men themselves, the breath and the blood in them, go still on their benches at a single word, because every one of them knows it. Every son of the north who has sailed west of the fjord knows the Hvítingar. The White Ones. Nine miles of skerries lying low in the grey sea west of the Hjaltland race like the teeth of a jaw that goes on and on, so low that at the top of the tide they are under the water entirely and at the bottom they stand up out of it white with the birds' shit and the bones of the birds and the broken strakes of every ship that ever tried them, and the sea between them never lies still. It runs. It boils. The tides come through the Hvítingar from three directions at once and fight each other among the stones so that the water goes white over them even on a calm day, and in autumn, in a wind, in a fog — no one takes a ship into the Hvítingar in autumn. No one takes a loaded fleet into them ever. My father lost two knarrs there before I was born. Hallr's eldest boy went down on them at nineteen with a Frisian trader he had signed on with for a summer, and in ten years at my oar Hallr has never once spoken of it, and I see him now at the loom with his straightened hands gone white on the wood, his face turned toward the starboard bow where his son's bones are, and his lips moving. I can hear it now under the oars. A sound like a great beast breathing through its teeth. The sea working on stone.

"We are inside them." Kaleb's voice has not changed. "We have been inside them a while, I think. That is why the water has felt strange under the keel. I thought it was her. It is the race." He turns to me, and he does not say *turn back*, and he does not say we will all die. I watch him not say it. I watch him look at you in my arms with your eyes closed and the fog pouring out of you and the marks at your ribs burning through the wool, and I watch something in him decide — the boy who was held over the side at nine. "She can see them?"

I look down at you. "Can you see them, kvinne?"

You do not open your eyes. "I see them," you say, from the bottom of the well. "The fog shows me. Every drop is an eye." Your voice drifts. "They are old. So old. Patient. They have been waiting since the sea came to them. They are *hungry*."

"Then she tells you, and you tell Hallr, and Hallr steers," Kaleb says. "No voices." He is already moving. He shoves the line into Jensen's hands — "*three, two, one long, many* — hold it, brother, feel it, do not let go of it" — and goes forward down the deck into the fog, fast and low, and I lose him, and then I see him again, a grey shape at the bow, climbing. Up onto the forward platform where Ásgeir stood. Past it. Onto the stem itself. He lies down along the top of the stem-post on his belly with his legs locked round the carved wolf's neck and his head out over the water, out beyond the prow, out into the white, looking down. And his right arm comes up behind him, back toward the stern, toward us, and holds there, raised.

And you begin to speak. The words come out of you soft and steady and far away, a woman reading something written on the inside of her own eyelids. "Port. A little. A little more." And I have moved — I have brought you in my arms the stride and a half to the oar so that the old man and I stand shoulder to shoulder with you between us — and I put my mouth by Hallr's ear and breathe it to him. "*Port. A little.*" Hallr leans on the loom and the *Úlfsvaka* swings, and out at the end of her, lying on the wolf's neck, Kaleb's raised arm goes to the left and holds. And I hear the sea breathing through its teeth on our starboard side, close, closer than any sea should breathe, and I look and the fog off the starboard quarter is lit from beneath by white water, boiling, churning over a black shape that comes up out of the sea a long oar's length from our strakes, black and slick and streaming, as high as a man and as broad as a hall, with birds' bones lodged in its cracks and a ship's rib — old, grey, bleached, the curved timber of some hull that came this way before us — wedged in a fissure of it like a bone in a dog's teeth. It goes by close enough to touch with an oar-blade. The men on the starboard benches ship their oars without being told and I hear the looms knock as they come in, and the rock slides past in the white like a great dark animal turning its head to watch us go.

"Starboard now." Your voice. "Hard. *Hard*."

"*Starboard hard*," I breathe, and Hallr throws his whole weight on the loom, and Kaleb's arm swings right, and the ship heels so far that the port gunwale goes down toward the water and the men on that side grab for the oar-ports, and the hull comes round — and something touches the keel. Every man aboard feels it. A long low grinding coming up through the planking into the soles of my feet, through the keel my father's first ship gave me, the sound of stone dragging along oak, slow, from the bow toward the stern, the whole hull shuddering with it, the strakes groaning, the clench-nails creaking in the planks. And you go rigid in my arms, every part of you at once, your back arching against my chest and your hands coming up — both of them, out of mine, out of the air — and *pushing*. Palms out. Down and out, toward the sea, toward the stone, your fingers spread and shaking, the cords standing out in your wrists, the whole small strength of you driving out through your written hands into the water the way a man drives his whole weight into a shield to hold a wall, and your mouth opens.

"Trust me," you whisper.

I do not know if it is to me or to the sea. Your nails have gone into my forearm where it is banded across you, I feel them break the skin, and I do not know if you are asking me or telling me or asking yourself, and it does not matter, because I do. I have nothing else. I cannot steer the ship. I cannot see the stones. I cannot cut the sea or the dead or the fog, and every blade I own is a dead weight on my hips, and the only thing on this ship I can do is hold the woman who is holding it, and so I hold. I set my feet. I put my whole body round you, my chest at your back and my arms round your waist and my thighs braced against the backs of yours and my mouth at your ear, and I give you my weight to push against, the way a man in the second rank puts his shoulder to the back of the man in front of him and *drives*, and I say into your ear the only thing I have. "I do. Push."

And you push. I feel it go out of you, all of it, something tearing loose in you and going out through your hands like a river through a broken dam, and the marks on your ribs flare so white that the light comes through the wool and the pelt both and lights the fog round us, and the marks at your nape go black, black so deep it is like looking down a well, and the sea under us *heaves*. It lifts the ship. The whole hull coming up a hand's breadth, two, the grinding under the keel stopping as the stone drops away beneath us, as though something under the water had put its shoulder to the *Úlfsvaka* and lifted her over the reef the way a man lifts a child over a puddle, and she comes down on the far side with a slap that throws spray over the gunwale and runs on. Free. And the sea round us changes. The boiling goes out of it. The water that was running three ways at once and fighting itself among the stones lies down on either side of us like a dog that has been told, and through the fog ahead I see it with my own eyes, and I will see it until I die — a channel. A road. A long smooth stretch of dark water lying flat and still between the boiling white on either hand, running west through the skerries, winding the way a path winds through a forest, and on either side of it the sea tearing itself to pieces on the stones, and in the middle of it the water as calm as a fjord on a summer evening, and exactly as wide as a ship.

Out on the wolf's neck, Kaleb's arm drops, and then comes up again, slowly, pointing straight ahead. Behind us, on Jensen's line, three tugs.

The fleet comes through behind us one by one, on the rope, down the road you made, and not one hull touches stone. It takes the rest of the light. I stand at the oar with Hallr's shoulder against mine and you in my arms with your hands out and shaking, and I breathe your words into the old man's ear — *port, steady, starboard a little, steady* — and he steers, and Kaleb lies out on the prow with his arm raised and the rain running down his back, and the skerries go by on either side of us in the white, black and streaming and hung with the bones of birds and men, close enough to touch, for nine miles. On every one of them, as it goes by, the white water climbs and boils and reaches for us, and falls back, as though it had come to the end of a chain. Halfway through, Hallr begins to weep. He does not stop steering. He does not make a sound. The tears run down into his beard and into the corner of his mouth, and he steers his ship through the stones that took his son, over the place where his son's bones are, on a road a woman made for him out of the sea, and when we pass the last skerry and come out into open water and the boiling falls away behind us, he lifts one hand off the loom for the first time since the frost touched it and lays it on top of your head, on the wet white hair, for the space of a breath, the way an old man puts his hand on a grandchild, and takes it away, and does not look at either of us.

The impossible has happened twice in four days. I have stopped keeping a tally of what is possible.

The crack of a hand on flesh comes down the deck out of the fog sometime after the last skerry, and with it the scrape of steel on wood, and with them both a woman's scream. It is so loud. After hours of no voice louder than a breath, after Kaleb and Jensen and I laughing without sound and Hallr weeping without sound and eleven hundred men passing a rope in silence, the scream goes up into the fog like a torch thrown into a dark hall, and every man on the ship flinches from it, and I feel the flinch go through you in my arms, and I feel something else — far back in the white behind us, the song *turning*.

Forward, at the thwart where Mildrith has been kept, there is a knot of grey shapes in the fog, and in the middle of it a man standing over a girl on her knees. Geirmund. I know him by the size of him before I see his face. He pulls the fifth oar on the port side and has for six years, a big slow dark man broad as a door, forty-four winters, with a scar through his left eye that the Danes gave him at Jelling and that he could not see out of until three days ago on a Saxon beach, and he is standing over Mildrith with his right hand still raised from the blow and his left hand pressed to his chest, and there is blood coming out from between its fingers, bright, running down over his belt. A cracked water-flask lies on the planking at his feet in a spreading dark puddle, and beside it, skittered off where his blow sent it, a knife. Mildrith is on her knees with her bound hands in front of her and her face bloodied, her lip split and the blood running down her chin onto the grey blanket, her yellow hair wild and wet and plastered to her skull, and her eyes are blazing. The rope at her wrists has been half sawn through, the strands frayed, and there is a ragged red welt across her palms where she held the blade by its edge to work it. Geirmund does not look at his wound. He looks at her. He has a face like a slab of granite and the eyes in it have always been mild, and they are mild now, looking down at a girl who has just put a knife in him, with something in them I am not close enough to read.

"Do not *touch* me, northern *dog*." She spits it in the broken Norse she has been learning with her ears pricked for three days, and then spits in fact, a gob of blood onto Geirmund's boots. "What did I say? What did I say that is not *truth*?" Her voice goes up and up, and the whole deck hears it, and past the deck the fog, and past the fog, I would swear on every god there is, the dead. "You all follow the witch blind like *fools*. She is *nothing*. She is nothing! She should have been strangled in the snow where she was found — my father *said* so, my mother said it every day of my life, they should have left her for the foxes — and you, you stupid dogs, you stupid *Northern* dogs, you have seen a few *parlor tricks* and now you are on your knees worshipping a *whore* —"

I see your face change. I am looking down at you because I am always looking down at you, and the fog is thinning round us now that you have let the Hvítingar go, and your eyes are open for the first time in hours, dazed and dimmed, the amber gone dull as old honey, and I watch the words go into you. *Strangled in the snow.* I watch them go in past the god and the fire and the war-marks and the stone you have built in yourself over twenty-one years, and find the place under all of it, and I watch your face — which has not moved for a sea or a god or a dead wolf — *move*. A tightening at the mouth. A thing at the eyes. So small that no one else on this ship would have seen it. It does not look like anger to me. It looks older than anger: the look of someone who has heard a thing said about her so many times that she stopped hearing it, and is hearing it again, and has no stone left to put in front of it because she spent all her stone on the sea.

And *whore*. That word was my mother's. Not her name — her word. The one the trueborn boys called her across the yards, and the women at the wells, and the thralls who had better places in the Hall than she did. The Jarl's whore. The Saxon whore. The one he had on her back in the brewhouse and never once looked at after. I was twelve when I took on every boy in the village for that word, and I have killed men for it since, and I have never once in sixteen years heard it said in Saxon. In her tongue, the tongue she sang to me in. And I hear it now out of a Saxon girl's bloody mouth about you, with your face changing in my arms, and the white does not climb. There is no ladder. It is simply there, all of it at once, from the floor of my gut to the top of my skull, and I am moving.

I do not let go of you. I want that set down, because I did not decide it and I do not know why. I go forward down the deck through the fog with my arm round your waist and you half carried and half dragged along with me, your bare feet stumbling on the planking, and I do not put you down and I do not slow. Men get out of my way. They see my face coming out of the fog and they get off their benches and out of the way the way men get out of the way of a ship coming in too fast to the dock, and I go through the gap they leave with Galdur beside me and his hackles up, and Kaleb behind me and Jensen behind him, and I reach the thwart, and I put out my free hand, and I take Mildrith by the throat. I lift her. Off her knees, off the deck, one-handed, the way I lifted Ásgeir against the mast, and she is lighter than Ásgeir, she is nothing, a sack of feathers with a pulse in it hammering against my palm, and I put her back against the thwart's upright and hold her there with her toes scrabbling at the planking and her bound hands clawing at my wrist and her blue eyes gone huge above my fingers, and the scream stops, because there is no air for it.

And I speak to her in Saxon. I do not decide that either. It comes up out of me with the white the way the word *mōdor* came up out of me in the fog, from the same place, from under everything. Not the trader's Saxon a Norseman picks up in ten summers of raiding, the words in the wrong order and the sounds all wrong in his mouth. The other one. The West Saxon of the chalk country. My mother's. The soft long vowels and the hard *c*'s and the little lilt at the end of a question that the Mercians laugh at. The tongue I spoke before I spoke Norse, in a kennel, with my face against a woman's arm, and have not let out of my mouth in front of another living soul in twenty-one years except on beaches, a word at a time, in a raider's accent, so that no man would ever know.

"My mother was called that," I tell her, very low, very clear, in the tongue of her own hearth. "In this tongue. By men with better teeth than yours." My fingers do not tighten. They do not need to. "They are all dead, little one. Every one of them. I was twelve when I started, and I did not stop until the last of them, and that was not so very long ago." I bring my face close to hers, close enough that she can see my eyes and nothing else. "You will not say it again. You will not say anything again. Not on this sea. Do you understand what is in that water? It is listening. It comes to a voice, any voice, and you have just screamed into the fog with the whole of the dead north turning its head to hear you, and if you open your mouth again before I give the word, I will not cut your throat. I will hold you over the side by your hair and let them come and take you, and I will watch them do it, and I will not lift a finger, and I will tell your mistress you slipped."

I watch her understand me. Every word. I watch her understand that the Wolf of the Fjord, the Northman, the heathen dog who burned her village, has just spoken to her in the soft Saxon of a West Country farm-wife, of a mother, and I watch the understanding turn into a thing I have only ever seen on the faces of men on beaches in the last breath before I kill them: the knowledge that the thing in front of them is not what they thought it was. And against my side, under my arm, I feel you go still. It is not the stillness of exhaustion. It is sharper. It is the stillness of someone laying one thing down beside another, and I can guess what you are laying it beside. A beach, an Elder on his knees in the mud speaking to the Wolf in Saxon — begging, bargaining, trading you for sixty lives, saying things about you in his own tongue, in front of you, that he thought the Northman could not follow — and the Northman listening, and saying nothing, and letting him think it. I do not look down at you. I cannot. If I look down I do not know what I will see.

"*Wolf*."

A hand on my forearm. No one puts a hand on my arm when the white is in it. Kaleb does, sometimes, and goes on the deck for it. Jensen did, in the fog, and I let him because there was no white in me then, only grief. This is a big slow scarred hand with blood on its knuckles and blood on its palm, and it closes on my forearm above the wrist where my hand holds Mildrith's throat, and does not pull. It only rests there, heavy as a stone. Geirmund. He stands at my shoulder with the blood running down his belly and his mild eyes on my face, and he says, in the slow plain voice I have heard perhaps fifty times in six years, because Geirmund does not speak unless a thing needs saying and a thing very rarely needs saying, "So. She was thirsty."

That is all. He does not take his hand off my arm.

"She put a knife in you," I say, in Norse. My voice comes out of me like gravel.

"So. She has small hands." Geirmund does not smile. Something moves in the granite that on another man would be one. "It is not deep. I have had worse from my wife, when she was alive. She threw a pot at me once, at Jól." His hand stays where it is. "I gave her water. She did not want it from me. That is all."

"You struck her."

"After." He looks at the girl, at the blood on her chin. "I am sorry for it. My hand is quicker than I am. It always was." A breath. "I buried two her size, Wolf. Four winters past. The coughing sickness, and my wife after them." He says it the way Hallr says weather. "I do not like to see one held up off the ground by the throat. Even one with a knife." He takes his hand away, slowly, and steps back, and does not lower his eyes. "That is all I have to say."

The white is still in me, roaring in every bone of my back. And there is a big slow man of forty-four winters standing in front of me with a hole in his chest that a girl made, asking me not to kill the girl — not asking, saying a thing and stepping back from it. I have known Geirmund six years. He has never once asked me for anything, not silver, not a better oar, not a woman off a beach, and I have never seen him do a cruel thing a fight did not ask of him, and I understand, with the girl's pulse slamming against my palm, that I am about to take something from him he does not have the words to ask for. I open my hand. Mildrith drops to the deck on her knees and folds over her bound hands, dragging air into her throat in great whooping gasps, and I stand over her and wait until the gasps go quiet, and they do go quiet, because she has understood me. She does not scream again. She does not say a word.

"Gag her," I say to the fog. "Her own hem. Tear it." Somebody does; I do not see who. "Bind her hands again, proper, behind her. And put her on the stem-post. Facing out."

"Brother." Kaleb, behind me, low. "Not the post."

I turn my head and look at him. He stands at my shoulder with his bitten hand wrapped in the black-stained rag and his face still wet from lying out on the prow in the rain, and he is not grinning. He is looking at Mildrith. He has been looking at her, I think, since the first hour on the beach when she threw the stone, and he has been told in front of the men, by me, what that look is worth, and he is giving it to her anyway, in front of me, in front of the men, with his face very steady.

"Facing out," I say. "Into the fog. She wants to call out to something so badly, let her look at what she would be calling."

"She is a child."

"She is a child who put a blade in Geirmund." I hold his eyes. "With a knife she did not have when we sailed from Merewic." And I look down at the deck, at the small blade lying in the puddle from the cracked flask, and see it properly for the first time. An eating knife. Short, single-edged, a plain iron blade and a handle of yellowed bone worn smooth, with a crack running down the bone that someone has bound with a twist of copper wire. I know that knife. I have seen it at supper for two summers, spearing mutton out of the pot. I have seen it turning in a man's fingers while he talked.

I lift my head and look down the deck into the thinning fog. Hauk is at the mast-step, three strides off at the edge of the knot of men, with his hands open at his sides and his face very calm. At his belt, where a knife-sheath hangs on every man in my crew, there is a small plain leather sheath, and it is empty.

"She took it off me." He says it before I can ask, fast and easy. Hauk has always had an easy mouth. "In the fog, Wolf, when we were among the stones. I heard her crying under the thwart like a child and I thought, the rope has cut her, she will lose the hands if it is too tight, and no one wants that, the god-born will want her thrall whole. So I went to look at her bonds. In the dark. And she —" He spreads his empty hands. "She was quick. I did not feel it go. You know how they are, the little ones, quick fingers. I did not know it was gone until I saw it in Geirmund."

He holds my eyes, and he is not afraid of me. That is the thing I mark. Every man on this deck is afraid of me with the white in my face, and Hauk stands three strides off with his empty sheath and his easy mouth and looks at me with his calm face, and he is not. I do not say anything to him. I look at him for a long time, long enough that the men round him begin to shift away from him a hair, the way men shift away from a man the Wolf has looked at for too long, and then I bend and take the knife out of the puddle and put it through my own belt at the back, beside the long seax. "I will keep this," I say, "until the god-born decides what it is owed." And I turn my back on him and feel his eyes on my back the whole way, and I put it with the thing I decided about him two nights past in the moonlight, and I tell no one.

They put Mildrith on the stem-post, facing out into the white, with her hands bound behind her round the carved wolf's neck and a strip of her own grey hem between her teeth and her yellow hair hanging wet down her back. She does not fight. She has gone quiet in a way that is not the quiet of a girl who has given up — I have seen that on a hundred beaches, and it is slack — but a quiet with something tight and hard in the middle of it, like a fist closing in the dark. She looks out into the fog, and the fog looks back. Kaleb goes forward after her. He does not ask me. He climbs onto the forward platform and sits down on the planking at the foot of the stem-post, at Mildrith's feet, with his back against the carved wood beside her bound hands and his axe across his knees and his face turned down the length of the ship toward me, and he sits there and looks at me, steadily, his grey eyes level. Not challenging. Not asking. The one man who will tell me I am wrong to my face, telling me so without a word, and staying where he is to say it. I let him.

Jensen has gone to Geirmund. He has torn the bottom off his own tunic and is pressing it to the big man's chest, and Geirmund stands for it patiently, the way an ox stands for the yoke, looking down at Jensen's hands, and when Jensen asks him something I do not hear, he says, "So. It is nothing. She missed the bone." And then, after a moment, looking toward the stem-post through the fog, so quietly I barely catch it: "My Ása would have done the same, at that age, if a man had burned her house."

Far behind us in the white, the song comes on. Louder. Nearer. Turned toward the place where a girl screamed. I feel it in the timbers before I hear it, in the cold coming up off the water, the chorus gathering under it, and I feel you hear it in my arms — and I feel you, emptied, swaying on your feet with your eyes half-closed, *reach*. Again. Down into whatever you have left. The fog thickens round us one more time and the shadow-ships go out into the white again and the song turns to follow them, and the cost of it goes out of you so hard that your knees buckle and I catch you, and a tremor goes through you that does not stop. Mildrith's scream cost you that. I put it with the rest.

I take you back to the furs. I sit with my back to the sternpost and you between my thighs, the way you have lain since the first night, with the wolf-pelt over us both and Galdur across your feet and Skuggi on my shoulder with his head pressed to my neck, and I hold you, and you do not stop shaking. It is not hard, the shaking. It is a fine steady tremor under the skin, like a bowstring drawn and loosed a hundred times that will not quite go still, and the marks at your ribs that burned white all afternoon are cold under my hand, cold and hollow, as though someone had scraped them out with a knife. The fog is thinning. You are not holding it now; you are only letting it go slowly enough that it does not tear. And I sit with you and do not speak, and I think.

I cannot put it in the place behind my teeth anymore. The place has been full since the comb and overflowing since the *Hrímfaxi*, and it all comes out now in the grey thinning light, with the oars going and the song faint and further behind us and your weight on my chest, and I sit there and let it come. My father sent me south. Halldis threw the bones in the spring and said the Wessex coast was soft and unwatched and the gods were looking away from it, and my father lifted two fingers at the Hall and said *south*, and I went. He did not tell me Merewic; I found Merewic. But he chose the season and the coast, and when I asked him which road home, he said the outer one, north along the coast past the Dogger and up, not the inner passage by the Danish islands, and I did not ask him why, because I never ask my father why. And the outer road is the road that brought me past the place where a dead ship was waiting with his dead brother's name on her. So I sit with you in my furs and I think: did he know. Did he know what was on that road. Did the one-eyed woman in the black hood throw the bones in the spring and see a grey ship in them and say nothing, and send the bastard home along the coast with eleven hundred men to see whether the dead would take him the way they took Steinn. And if they had, who would have been left at his right hand. Who would have been left on the red heather. Only one dragon.

And under that, worse, in the place where I do not go: *two dragons fight on a grave*. Hákon's face across the red heather of the old Jarl's howe with a blade in his hand, and in me, nothing. The nothing that was cold and calm and had been waiting. I felt it on the deck with the god's voice going into the whole fleet, and I have felt it every hour since. A man should feel something at the thought of killing his father's son. Even a bastard should. Even a thing with teeth. I look for it and there is nothing there, only the cold and the calm, and I do not know if that is the bravest thing in me or the most rotten, and it is eating me from the inside like a worm in a ship's timber, slowly, where no one can see.

"You are not wrong to not feel."

Your voice scrapes. It comes out of you so hoarse and so worn that it is barely more than breath, and it goes into me like a blade into a seam I did not know was open. I look down. You have turned your head on my chest and you are looking up at me with your eyes open, dim and weary, the amber gone to the color of honey left too long in the pot, but present. Seeing me. And I go still. My whole body stops, my breath and my hand on your belly and my heart for a beat, the way a man stops when he feels a hand go into his purse in a crowded market. You did not ask. I did not speak. I sat here thinking in the dark behind my own teeth, the way I have thought every hard thing in my life, alone, where no one can reach — and you have reached. You have walked into the one room in me that never had a door, and you are standing in it, looking at me, and I do not know what my face is doing. Your hand comes up, slowly, shaking, and finds my jaw the way mine finds yours, and cups it, the small cold palm along the bone under the beard.

"The black and the dragons fighting." Your voice scrapes, and holds. "You did not flinch at the thought of killing your brother, and that is eating at you now."

My face does something. I do not know what.

"The ambush was designed by no mortal man." You do not look away. "And you are not wrong for being the son on the right, Loki. He was born and handed the left. You fought, and became the right."

The green flares in my eyes. I feel it — the white coming up, the hot one, at being seen, at being *read*, at a woman walking into my skull without knocking and turning over the one thing in it I would have cut out with my own seax before I showed anyone — and I feel my hand close on your belly and my jaw go to iron under your palm, and you do not flinch. I see you not flinch. You lie in my arms emptied and shaking and you look up into the face that made you flinch over the basin two days ago, and you hold my eyes, and you go on. "Your father did not send you to the village either. I am not all-knowing, but there are things I do know, and this is one of them." Your thumb moves on my jaw, once. "He did not send you here to die."

Your voice drops into the place it has been going these three days when it is only for me, the place your hands go when they are on my shoulders and your body goes when it is under mine in the furs, and I can read on your face that you would have said it even if I struck you for it. That there will be nothing but truth out of you for as long as you draw breath, and if the truth earns you the back of my hand you will take the back of my hand and give me the truth again. I watch you lie there and look at the hand on your belly that could break you and decide that it does not matter. And under that I see a thing I have never once seen on any face turned toward mine — not in the yards, not in the Hall, not in nine winters of Ylva. You have nothing left in you, the cold where your marks were, my blood under your nails, and you have spent the last of what you have on this. On my hurt. As though my hurting were a thing that hurt you. I have no shelf for that. So I do the only thing I know how to do with a thing I cannot hold, and go at it straight.

"You were in my head." It comes out low and rough and not quite steady. "I did not open it to you."

"No." You do not look away. "I did not reach for it. Every door in me is open tonight. I cannot shut them. It came in anyway." A breath. "I will not do it on purpose."

"You ask first. Always." I hear my own words from the furs two mornings gone, and I see you hear them. Then, because it is in my mouth, and because you have just lain there and told me you would take my hand across your face for the truth, and I cannot leave that lying between us on the furs like a dropped blade: "And you can stop bracing for my hand, kvinne." Your eyes flicker. "I have hit women for lying to me. I have hit them for stealing from me and for crying when I wanted quiet and for being in reach when the white came up. I will not pretend to you I have not, and I will not pretend I am sorry for every one, because I am not." My jaw works under your palm. "I have never once in my life hit a woman for telling me the truth. None of them ever told it to me long enough to find out what I would do. You do not lie to me. I have watched for it, and you do not. So the back of my hand is not a thing you need to carry. Not for this. Not for anything you say to me true." A breath. "Set it down."

You do not answer. But your hand on my jaw, which had been cupping it braced against something, goes soft, and stays.

I look away from you, out over the gunwale at the grey thinning fog. I cannot say the next thing to your face. "When Hákon was five," I say, "he came to the kennel." I have never told this, not to Kaleb, not to Jensen, who was there and might remember. It comes out of me in the flat voice I use for the count. "I was nine. Two winters after my mother. I slept with the dogs because no one had told me I could sleep anywhere else. And one night in the spring the trueborn boy came down from the Hall in his good wool with his black hair combed, and stood in the kennel door in the dark with a honey-cake in his fist. From the Jarl's table. Still warm." I can smell it, twenty years on. "He held it out. He did not say anything. He was five. He held it out to the thrall's whelp in the straw because I looked hungry, I suppose, and he did not know yet that he was not supposed to see me. I took it. I ate it in two bites in front of him with the dogs watching, and he smiled at me, and went back up to the Hall." The oars go, and go. "His mother found the crumbs on his tunic. She had him whipped in the yard the next morning in front of the household, for going to the kennel. Twelve strokes. He was five. I watched from the kennel door. He did not come again. And by the time he was ten, he came at night with the others. From behind. With a stick."

Your thumb moves on my jaw. You do not say anything.

"So you see." I make myself look down at you. "It is not that there is nothing in me for him. I have killed him every night since I was twelve. In my head, in the dark, with my back to the kennel wall. A thousand times. With my hands, with stones, with my first blade and every blade after. I have killed him so many times, kvinne, that when the god showed me the red heather there was nothing left to feel. It was already done." My voice goes rough. "And I have never once let myself remember that he was five, and that he brought me the cake. Not once. Until now." I stop. I cannot go on with that. I look away again. "And my father. No. He did not send me to die." My mouth moves, and it is not a smile. "He did not send me at all. He lifted two fingers and said *south*, and I went, the way the dogs go when he whistles. To send a man to his death you have to think about him first."

The fog is almost gone. The light comes through it grey and thin, and your hand stays on my jaw, and I let it, and neither of us says anything else.

The horn comes out of the north. Far away, so far that I hear it more in the hull than in the air, a long low note coming across the water from somewhere behind us and to the east, and it is no horn I have ever heard. It is not the cow-horn of a fishing boat or the bronze lur of a Jarl's ship or the hunting horn of the Saxon thegns. There is no music in it. It has rhythm — a long note and a pause, a long note and a pause, steady as a heart and slower than any heart — but nothing a man could hum, and under the note, or inside it, a thing I have no word for, as though the horn were being blown by something with the shape of a man and no breath in it. As though the sound were being *pushed* through the bone by something other than air. It is calling. I know that the way I knew the stopping-place of my mother's song. It is calling across the water in a tongue older than oars, and it is not calling to us.

You lift your head and turn your face north toward the sound, and your eyes go distant the way they go when you are listening to something under the world. "They have turned," you breathe. The horn calls again, fainter, further east. "They are following the ships I made. North and east, into the haze. Hunting the shadows." Your head drops back against my chest. "They do not know where we are. We are safe. For now."

The word goes down the deck without anyone saying it aloud. It goes from face to face in the thinning fog the way the god's hand went, and I watch shoulders come down that have been at men's ears for hours, and somewhere forward a man lets out a long ragged breath that is nearly a sob, and stops it.

You want to stand. I feel you want it, and I do not ask why; I get up and bring you with me to the stern rail, and you put your hands on the oak, and the fog goes. It does not lift. It comes apart. Without you holding it, it peels back off the sea in long ragged strips the way old paint peels off a shield-boss, and through the gaps the world comes back. Grey sky, low and heavy, the black of the storm gone out of it and only the plain iron grey of an autumn evening left. Black water — not dead water, but running, moving, alive, a long slow swell coming in from the west that lifts the hull and lets it down, and the wind fresh and cold and smelling of salt and weed and nothing else. And behind us on the rope, coming out of the last of the white one after another, my fleet. The *Skarfr*. The *Járnkjaftr*. The *Gjallar*. The wolf-prows and the sworn ships and the fat knarrs at the tail, every one of them still tied to the next, a long necklace of hulls on a hemp cord strung out across the grey water, and on every deck men standing at the rails staring at the sky as though they had forgotten it. And to the west, low on the horizon, a dark line. Cliffs. Black. I do not know them.

Your legs go. I catch you, my arm round your waist hauling you up against me before your knees touch the planking, and you are so light. You hang in my arm and I feel your body protest, every muscle in it trembling, the marks along your ribs cold and hollow under my hand where they burned white all day, and the heat that was in your spine through every hour of the fog gone out of it. In its place there is a different heat, low and deep and wrong, a fever-heat coming up from somewhere inside you in slow waves. And the shadow-marks at the nape of your neck — I am looking, I see them — are not burning. They are lying dark and still against the white skin the way a dog lies by the hearth when it has finally been fed, and every hair on my body stands up at it, because I have seen that stillness before. On the beach, on Galdur, with the boar's blood still on his muzzle.

"We are safe." Your voice rasps against my chest. "They bought the illusion of the fleet and followed it." A breath. "Your men are whole."

I cannot answer you.

And then you turn your head, the way you turned it on the beach when the pillar went out, and look at the deck. They are all looking at you. Forty-six men on their feet, silent. Torvald with the marlinspike still in his fist. Ásgeir at the bow with his arms at his sides. Hallr at the loom with his wet face. Sven. Brandr with the horn he did not blow. Ketill with his hand on his chest where his hammer is not. Geirmund with Jensen's rag pressed to his ribs. Hauk at the mast-step, his face calm. And Kaleb at the stem-post with Mildrith's bound feet beside his shoulder and his axe across his knees. I feel you brace in my arms. I know that brace now; I have watched it for four days. You brace for what you have always found in faces turned toward you, and it is not there. I see you see that it is not there. I watch you look along the deck from face to face and find on every one of them a thing that was not there in the mud, and it is not worship — you told them not to, and they have listened — and it is not fear. It is heavier and quieter than either. The look of men who were in the fog with you, who heard the shadow-ships speak with their own voices and felt the sea lift them over the stones, and know that every one of them would be on the bottom of the Hvítingar or in the cold arms of the dead if a woman had not stood at the stern of the ship and poured herself into the sea until she could not stand. The look a shield-wall gives the man who held the gap.

Brandr lifts the hand that held the horn and puts his fist on his heart. Then Sven. Then Torvald. Then Ketill and Geirmund and Ásgeir and the men on the benches, one after another down the length of the deck, every fist going to every heart without a sound, and at the steering oar Hallr takes one hand off the loom and does it, and at the stem-post Kaleb lays down his axe and does it, and does not take his eyes from me while he does.

You turn your face into my throat. I feel the wet, hot against my skin under the beard, the first tears you have let go since the red thread, and you hide them there in the hollow of my throat where you hid your face on the beach, and you do not lift your head, and I put my hand on the back of your skull over the braid and turn my body so that it is between you and the deck, and let you hide. Skuggi shifts on my shoulder, his wings rustling, and turns his head to look down at you with one black eye, close, and makes a sound in his chest I have not heard him make, low and soft, almost a croon. And Galdur pushes his great head up under your hand where it hangs at your side, and when you try to take it away he pushes harder, and holds it there.

"We need shelter," you whisper against my skin. I feel your lips move on my throat. "Land. Soon. Long enough to let the dead give up the loss of their prey. The sea says the water is not safe yet. And I need —" Your fingers curl into my tunic. "Loki. I need earth. The wild. Away from any village, away from any threat. I need it to come back, if I am to keep us safe."

"Skógey."

Jensen, before I can open my mouth. He has come aft without a sound, the way he does, and he stands a pace off with Geirmund's blood on his hands and his face still ash-pale from the day — the god and the storm and the word he broke his grandmother's oath to say — and his voice is steady and his dark eyes are on you, with the look they get when he has been running the count in his head a long while and has arrived at a number he does not like. Your hands tighten on my chest, and you lift your head from my throat. "What is Skógey?" Your voice cracks. You look from Jensen to me to the stem-post, where Kaleb has come to his feet.

"It is madness." Kaleb comes down the deck. He leaves Mildrith at the stem-post with a look at the men nearest her that keeps them where they are, and comes aft and stops at the seam in the planking, at the line, and crosses his arms over his chest. His face is the split rock he accused me of. "That is what it is. Utter fucking madness."

He is not wrong. Every man who has sailed west of Hjaltland knows Skógey the way he knows the Hvítingar, from the stories told at the far end of the Hall after the mead. Two islands, low and green, lying a day west of the race where no islands have any right to be, with a narrow sound between them like the parting of a woman's hair. And on them the forest. Not birch scrub, not the stunted rowan and juniper that clings to every other rock in these waters — forest, old and dense, trees so thick at the root that four men linking hands cannot reach round them, growing down to the edge of the water so that their roots go into the sea, trees that were old when my father's grandfather's grandfather split his first keel-timber. A stone circle in the middle of it, the old men say, that no man living has seen, carved all over with marks in no tongue anyone can read. And something else. That is where the stories go quiet and the old men look into the fire. Something lives on Skógey. Something that was there before the Norse came and before the Picts and before whoever came before the Picts. It has troubled no one in living memory. It has not needed to, because no one goes. The stories say it belongs to the Old World the way the white country belongs to it, and the Old World does not like visitors.

"My grandfather's time." Kaleb says it to you, not to me. "Ásbjörn Skerry-Crow of Hjaltland put in at Skógey with three ships for water. In the spring, in fair weather, sixty men." His jaw is tight. "He came out with three ships and sixty men. Every one. Not a scratch on any of them." He lets it sit. "And not one of them would say what they had seen. Not drunk, not dying, not under the whip. Ásbjörn gave his ships to the Jarl of Orkney the same summer and went to live in a cave on Fetlar like a hermit, and when men went to ask him why, all he would say was that the trees had *watched him sleep*." He looks at me. "We are not going to Skógey, brother. Not with her like this. Not with a fleet. Not with the dead behind us. There are the Orkneyjar. There is Foula. There are a hundred rocks between here and the fjord where a fleet can lie up and fill its casks and nothing will watch it sleep."

"The Orkneyjar are two days south and full of the Jarl of Orkney's men," Jensen says, steadily, "who will count our hulls and send a fast boat to every hall from here to Jutland to say the Wolf came west with a god-born in his stern. Foula is a bare rock with no water and no wood and no shelter for a fleet. And she said *earth*, Kaleb. She said *the wild*." He turns to me. "There is nowhere wilder between here and the white country than Skógey. And it is a day's run. Less, with this wind."

He is right. I feel it on my own face, and I see you see it there. It is the closest land. The sound between the islands is deep and sheltered and would swallow the whole fleet from any eye on the open sea. There is fresh water, every story says so, streams coming down cold and clean out of the trees. There is wood. There is game, deer as tall as horses that no one hunts. There is rest. And Kaleb, who hates it, has already counted it in his head — I can see the count on him — a day to Skógey, three days lying up, four days home round the north of Hjaltland and down the coast with a fair wind. We would come into the fjord in eight days, and Winter Nights is in eleven. The oath-day. The day every sworn captain in my father's fleet climbs the hill to the Great Hall and kneels at the Seat and puts his hands between my father's and swears himself again for another year, and takes a ring for it, while the whole north watches. Thorolf will be there. Bersi, with his daughter promised. Every captain on this water who felt the god's hand and heard you say *he is my choice*. We would come in with three days to spare.

"Why Skógey," I say to Jensen. "Why not any rock. You have a reason. You have had it in your mouth since the stones."

Something goes across his face that I have seen there twice in my life — once on the beach, and once tonight in the fog with the word on his lips — the look of a man about to say a thing he is not sure he is allowed to say. "*Halls of redwood long forgotten*," he says.

The deck goes quiet.

"The trees on Skógey are not pine and they are not oak," Jensen says. "The stories all say it, and no one ever listens to that part. They are red. Red bark. Red wood under it, red as heart's-blood, that weeps red sap when you cut it, and dark needle-leaves that stay green through the winter." He looks at you. "Yew. The old men say the oldest yews were growing before the gods made men out of driftwood on the shore. Gróa said Yggdrasil itself is no ash at all — that the old songs call it *the needle-ash*, and the needle-ash is the yew, the tree that does not die, that rots out from the heart and grows again round the hollow and goes on. And she said —" He stops. His hand goes to his hammer. "She said that Ullr, the hunter, the god of the bow and the snowshoe and the winter, has his hall in a place called Ýdalir. Yew-Dales. And that it is not in Ásgarðr with the others. That it was on the earth once, at the edge of the sea, in the west, before the gods went up, and that the children of —" His voice catches. "She did not finish it. She was very old. She was singing it, at the end, and she did not finish the verse, and I never asked her what the rest was, because I was a boy and I thought there would be time." His eyes are wet again. He does not hide it. "*Children of the forest dancing in halls of redwood.* The Allfather put it in her mouth this morning in front of eleven hundred men, Loki, and then the sea brought us out of the stones a day's run from the only forest of red trees in the western ocean. I do not know what is on Skógey. I do not want to know. But I do not think we were brought this way by chance, and I do not think it is a place we are meant to sail past."

Kaleb swears, long and low and vicious, the way he swears when he knows he has lost, a string of oaths about Jensen's mother and Jensen's grandmother and every yew tree ever grown, and when he is done he looks at you, and his face goes quiet. "And if the trees watch her sleep?"

You open your mouth to answer him, and you cough. It comes up out of you without warning, a small dry cough, the kind a woman makes at a hearth when the smoke turns, and then another behind it that is not small and not dry, that comes up from deep in your chest and bends you forward over my arm, and you put your hand to your mouth and I put mine over it, and the cough goes on, wet, tearing; I feel it in my own ribs through your back. And when it stops and you sag against me breathing in short shallow pulls, I take my hand away from your mouth and look at it.

It is red.

Bright and wet, the whole of my palm, the calluses and the old scars and the lines of it red with the blood that came out of you, running down my wrist, warm. I look at it for a long time. And I look at your mouth, and your lips are red with it, and there is a thread of it on your chin, and your eyes come up to mine above it, dim and honey-dark and swimming, and what I read in them is not fear. You were afraid this morning. What I read in them now, with my hand full of your blood, looks like apology.

There is no choice to make. Kaleb sees the blood and the argument goes out of his face like a sail losing the wind. Jensen sees it and closes his eyes. And I do not remember the next part clearly. The men tell me I did not raise my voice, and I believe them. I remember the blood running warm down my wrist and your weight in my arm, and the white, the whole of it, all at once, and it did not go up my spine. It went down. Into my feet. Into the deck. Into the keel. And I remember my own voice, low and very level, the voice I use on beaches in the last moment before the killing starts: "Skógey. Kaleb, you have the course. Hallr, you have the oar. Jensen, the line — tell them on the rope. Every hull. Every oar. *Now*." And I remember that no one argued.

The *Úlfsvaka* comes round under my feet, her bow swinging from the black cliffs in the west to a point a little north of them where there is nothing but grey sea and grey sky, with Kaleb at the bow with his hand up giving Hallr the line, and the sail coming round on the yard with a crack, and the oars biting, and behind us on the rope — I hear it, three tugs and three and three, going back along the line from hull to hull — the fleet comes round after us. I sit down on the furs with you. I do not let anyone else hold you. Jensen comes and kneels and I let him put his hand on your forehead and his ear to your chest, because he has his grandmother's knowing and I have none, and he listens a long time with his eyes closed, and when he sits back on his heels his face is very careful. "It is not her lungs," he says. "There is no rattle. It is not a wound." He looks at the cold marks along your ribs where the wool has been scorched brown. "It is the cost. She spent past what she had, and the body is paying the rest. Gróa said that is how it goes with the ones who work the deep seiðr. When they run dry, they begin to burn the house to keep the hearth going. She has to stop spending. And she has to have the earth to fill her again." His mouth tightens. "Gróa always said it. *The sea gives and the sky gives, but the earth is the one that gives back.*"

"Then she will have earth." I do not look away from your face. You are not asleep; your eyes are half-open and on me, and every breath you take catches. "She will have the whole of an island." You try to say something, and I put my thumb on your lips. They are red and dry and cold. "No. Not now. You have spent all your words. You spent them on the sea and the stones and the shadow-ships and on me, and you will not spend another until you are on your earth with your hands in it." I take the water-flask from Brandr, who has come aft with it held out in both hands and his young face white, and I wet the corner of my tunic and wipe the blood from your mouth, slowly, and from your chin, and you let me. "You will lie in my furs and let me carry you. And I will carry you."

I hold you all the night. The wind holds behind us. It does not drop and it does not veer; it stays steady and hard out of the east the whole night through, and I do not think it is a wind that came on its own. The sky clears toward midnight and the stars come out hard and close the way they came out the night I held your hand to my mother's comb, and I look up at them and think of a hand on the back of a boy's neck, holding him over the side of a boat. Kaleb does not leave the bow. Twice in the night he comes aft to look at you, and stands at the seam with his arms crossed and his eyes on your face a long time, and goes back without a word. The second time I see him stop at the stem-post on his way forward and bend and do something with Mildrith's gag that I cannot see, and when he straightens she is drinking from the flask in his hand, and he holds it for her until she is done and puts the gag back, gently, and sits down again at her feet. I let him.

Jensen sits by my knee with his back against the sternpost and his hand in Galdur's ruff, and toward the middle of the night he begins, very softly, to sing. It is not a war-song or one of the lays. It is a thing in an old slow measure with words so archaic I can follow only one in three, about a woman who went into the earth in winter and came up out of it in spring, and as he sings I feel your breath even out against my chest and the catch go out of it, a little, and you sleep. He sings it three times through. When he stops, I see that he is weeping again. "Gróa's," he says, when he sees me looking. "She sang it over my mother when she was dying." He wipes his face with the back of his hand, the first time I have ever seen him wipe it. "It did not work for my mother."

"It is working now," I say.

He looks at you, asleep with your face in my throat. "Yes," he says. "I think it is."

At dawn we raise Skógey. I see it first, because I have not closed my eyes. Two humps on the horizon in the grey light, low and dark — not black like the cliffs to the south, but dark the way deep green goes dark in poor light, the way a forest goes dark at dusk — and as we come on they grow and grow until I can see two islands lying close together with a narrow gleam of water between them, and every inch of them from the shore to their high round crowns is trees. I have never seen so many trees in one place, not in the Frankish forests or the deep woods of Mercia or the pine-forests up the fjord behind my father's hall. They come down to the water's edge on every side, a wall of them so thick and close that I cannot see between them, and they are red. The bark, where the light finds it as we come in close, is a deep rusted red-brown like old iron, like dried blood, flaking in long strips, and the trunks are enormous, wider than a longship is long, some of them, fluted and twisted and split and grown back together, great columns of red wood going up into a roof of needles so dark green it is nearly black. And they are old. I can feel it the way you feel age in a very old hall, in the smoke-black of the rafters and the wear of the hearth-stones. These trees are so old that they make my father's Hall feel like a child's toy. The smell comes out to us across the water before we reach the sound. Resin. Earth. Wet moss. Something sweet under it, rich and dark, like honey left to ferment, and something older under that which I have no name for, and which makes the back of my neck prickle under the god's hand.

There are no birds. I notice it as we come into the sound between the islands. The sea-birds that have followed the fleet since Merewic, the gulls and gannets and skuas wheeling and screaming over the knarrs for scraps, are gone, and I did not see them go. And on the islands there is nothing. No crow, no thrush, no wood-pigeon clattering up out of the trees at the sound of oars. The forest is utterly silent. The only sound in the sound is our oars dipping and the water running off them and the slow creak of hulls as the whole fleet comes in behind us on the rope, one after another, into the narrow water between two walls of red trees, and the silence closing over us like a hand. Skuggi has made no sound since dawn. And Galdur stands at the bow beside Kaleb with his forefeet on the gunwale and his nose up, and he is not growling. His hackles are flat. His tail is low, very low, and still. And as the first of the trees comes close enough that its red roots go down into the water a long oar's length from our strakes, he makes a sound I have never heard from him — a whine, small and high in his throat, the sound a dog makes at a door it once lived behind and has not passed through in a very long time.

The water in the sound is warm. I put my hand over the side and feel it, warm like a bath, like the springs men speak of in Ísland where the earth breathes up through the rock, and a thin white steam lifts off the surface all down the sound, drifting among the hulls, curling round the red roots at the water's edge. Hallr sees it. Hallr sees everything. He says nothing.

There is a beach at the head of the sound, a crescent of grey shingle with the red trees standing back from it in a half-ring like the benches of a hall drawn back from the hearth, and moss running down from under them to the stones, deep green, thick as fur. Kaleb brings us in. I feel the keel touch shingle, gently, the way a hand touches a sleeping face, and I stand with you in my arms and I do not wait for anyone. I step over the gunwale with you against my chest and go down into the warm water to my thighs and wade, and come up out of it onto the stones of Skógey with the water running off my breeches and you in my arms, and I am the first. The shingle crunches under my boots, and then the moss, soft and deep and springing back under my weight, and I walk up it toward the trees, toward the biggest of them, a red giant at the center of the half-ring so huge that its trunk is split at the base into a great hollow, a doorway as high as a man and as wide as two, with the red heartwood inside it glowing in the grey light like the inside of a hall with the hearth lit. I kneel at the foot of it in the moss, and I lay you down.

Earth. I put you on the earth. On your back, on the moss, with the red roots on either side of you and the pelt open round you, and I take both your hands, the cold written palms, and turn them over and press them down into the moss, into the black soil under it, wet and cool and smelling of every autumn there has ever been, until your fingers sink in to the second knuckle and the dirt is under your nails. And I kneel over you with my hands on your wrists holding them there, and I watch your face. Your fingers curl into the earth, slowly, the way a sleeping child's fingers curl round a thumb. And the forest breathes. I do not know how else to say it. A long slow movement comes through the trees from deep in the island toward the beach — there is no wind, the east wind that drove us all night died at the mouth of the sound — a sigh going through the dark roof of the forest from one end of the half-ring to the other, every needle on every red tree trembling once, and going still, and the smell of the place rising round us thick and sweet and dark. Behind me at the water's edge I hear my men coming off the ships, the splash and crunch of boots in shingle, and then I hear them stop.

I look up. There are footprints in the moss. I did not see them when I walked up, because I was looking at the trees; I see them now, running from the edge of the shingle up the moss past where I kneel and on into the dark between the red trunks, and they are fresh, the moss still springing back up in them. Small prints. Bare. Many of them, a dozen, twenty, more, overlapping and crossing and running together the way children's feet run when they are playing, and they are the size of a child's foot, and the toes are too long. They go into the trees. None of them come out.

My hands are on my seaxes. I have drawn them both, the long and the short, the first steel I have drawn since the frost went up the blade on my own deck, and I know as I draw them that they may mean nothing here, the same as they meant nothing there, and I draw them anyway, because there is nothing else in me, and I kneel astride you on the moss of Skógey with a blade in each fist and my back to the red hollow and my face to the dark between the trees. Galdur comes up off the beach. He walks past me through the moss with his tail low and his ears forward and stops at the edge of the trees, at the place where the footprints go in, and looks into the dark. And from the dark, from very close, from just behind the nearest red trunk, close enough that I could reach it in three strides, comes a laugh — small and high and delighted, the laugh of a child who has been hiding and has been found — and Galdur's tail moves once, slowly, against the moss.
