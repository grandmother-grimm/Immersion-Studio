# The prose filter

A copy of Victoria's prose-decontaminator skill, kept in the studio so it loads in every session whether or not the account skill is present. It is a standing filter on every in-scene turn and on any prose written out of scene.

## The patterns

This skill is a standing filter. Apply it to every piece of prose you generate or edit. The patterns below are
the fingerprints of machine-generated text. Each one, used once, might pass unnoticed. Used habitually, they
flatten voice, kill surprise, and mark the writing as synthetic. The goal is not robotic avoidance but genuine
originality — writing that could only have come from a mind making real choices.

## How to Use This Skill

**During generation:** Before finalizing any prose output, mentally scan it against the patterns below.
If you catch one, rewrite the sentence. Do not just delete — *replace with something specific and alive.*

**During editing:** When reviewing or revising someone's draft, flag these patterns with concrete alternatives.

**The core principle:** Every sentence should earn its place through *specificity, surprise, or emotional precision.*
If a sentence could appear in any story, any context, any voice — it belongs in none of them.


## I. STRUCTURAL PATTERNS

These are the skeletal habits that make AI prose feel templated.

### 1. Negative Parallelism — "It's Not X, It's Y"

The construction that reframes by negation. Feels profound, delivers nothing.

**Contaminated:**
- "It's not a threat. It's a promise."
- "This isn't a setback — it's a beginning."
- "She wasn't angry. She was terrified."

**Why it fails:** It signals a revelation but the "reveal" is usually obvious. The negation adds words without
adding meaning. The reader already knew.

**What to do instead:** State the truth directly, or earn the contrast through action and context, not syntactic formula.
- "She wasn't angry. She was terrified." → "Her hands shook — not fists, just trembling open palms."
  (Now we SEE the distinction instead of being told it.)


### 2. Tricolon and the Tyranny of Threes

Three adjectives. Three verbs. Three examples. Three beats in every emotional moment. AI defaults to three
the way a metronome defaults to tempo — mechanically, without thought.

**Contaminated:**
- "She was smart, resourceful, and relentless."
- "He needed time, space, and silence."
- "The city was alive with music, laughter, and light."

**Why it fails:** Threes create a false sense of completeness. They round off the thought before the reader
has time to feel it. Real observation is ragged — sometimes one detail is enough, sometimes five.
The number should come from the content, not from habit.

**What to do instead:** Use the number the thought actually demands. One precise detail often hits harder
than three generic ones. If you write three things, make sure each one earns its place independently —
not as filler to complete a rhythm.
- "The city was alive with music, laughter, and light." → "Somewhere below, a trumpet player was
  murdering a Coltrane solo and didn't care who heard."
  (One specific, imperfect detail. Alive.)


### 3. Gerund Fragment Litany

Stacking gerunds as if rhythm alone creates meaning.

**Contaminated:**
- "Building. Breaking. Becoming."
- "Running through streets. Chasing something unnamed."
- "Watching, waiting, wanting."

**Why it fails:** It mimics intensity without earning it. The fragments feel cinematic but deliver no
information. They are atmosphere without substance — fog machines in an empty room.

**What to do instead:** Complete the thought. Who is building? What breaks? Be concrete. If the fragment
form genuinely serves the pace (rare), use ONE, not a chain.


### 4. The Rhetorical Self-Question — "X? A Y."

Posing a question and immediately answering it with a poetic reframe.

**Contaminated:**
- "A city of glass? A monument to ambition."
- "Love? A slow unraveling."
- "The truth? Something none of them were ready for."

**Why it fails:** It performs contemplation without actually thinking. The question is rhetorical, the
answer is predetermined, and the reader gains nothing. It is the prose equivalent of asking yourself
a question on stage and nodding wisely.

**What to do instead:** If the metaphor is good enough, state it directly. If the question matters, let
it remain unanswered. Genuine questions create tension; fake ones destroy it.


### 5. The Theatrical One-Liner Paragraph

A single short sentence isolated as its own paragraph for "impact."

**Contaminated:**
- "And then he was gone."
- "She didn't look back."
- "Nothing would ever be the same."

**Why it fails:** Used once in a chapter, it can land. Used every few paragraphs, it becomes a tic.
The isolation is supposed to create weight, but repetition makes it weightless. When everything is
emphasized, nothing is.

**What to do instead:** Earn the isolation. A one-line paragraph should arrive after sustained buildup,
not as a default paragraph-ending move. Limit to one per scene at most, and make sure the sentence
itself is strong enough to bear the white space.


### 6. The Echo/Mirror Construction

Two sentences that mirror each other for false symmetry.

**Contaminated:**
- "She was the storm. He was the silence after."
- "He built walls. She burned them down."
- "Where she ended, he began."

**Why it fails:** It reduces complex relationships to bumper-sticker duality. Real people are not
complementary halves of a metaphor.

**What to do instead:** Show the dynamic through action, contradiction, or a moment that resists
tidy summary. Let the reader feel the tension without the diagram.


### 7. The False-Profound Closing Fragment

Ending a paragraph or section with a fragment meant to resonate.

**Contaminated:**
- "Because that's what love was."
- "And maybe that was enough."
- "Always had been."

**Why it fails:** It asks the reader to feel something the prose hasn't earned. The fragment leans on
implied depth without creating actual depth. It is a gesture toward meaning, not meaning itself.

**What to do instead:** End on a concrete image, an action, or a line of dialogue. Let the reader
supply the resonance. Trust them.


## II. WORD-LEVEL CONTAMINATION

Specific words and phrases that signal AI origin.

### 8. The Usual Suspects — Overused AI Vocabulary

These words appear at vastly higher frequency in AI-generated text than in skilled human writing.
Their presence is a fingerprint. Avoid or severely limit:

- **delve / delved** — almost never appears in natural human prose
- **tapestry** (as metaphor) — "a tapestry of experience/emotion/culture"
- **landscape** (as metaphor for non-physical domains) — "the landscape of grief"
- **nuanced** — especially "a nuanced understanding"
- **multifaceted** — see also: "multifaceted approach"
- **testament** — "a testament to her resilience"
- **embark** — "embark on a journey"
- **foster** — "foster growth/connection/understanding"
- **leverage** (as verb outside finance)
- **moreover / furthermore / additionally** — as paragraph openers
- **realm** — "in the realm of"
- **myriad** — especially "a myriad of"
- **endeavor**
- **pivotal**
- **beacon**
- **resonate / resonated**
- **underscore**
- **navigate** (metaphorical) — "navigate grief," "navigate complexity"
- **juxtaposition**
- **interplay**
- **overarching**
- **simply put**
- **at its core**
- **in essence**

**What to do instead:** Use the word the specific context demands. If you reach for "navigate" to describe
someone dealing with grief, ask: what are they actually doing? Drinking alone at 2 AM? Calling their
mother and hanging up before she answers? That is the sentence.


### 9. The "Something" Placeholder

Using "something" as a stand-in for the emotion or observation the writer hasn't bothered to identify.

**Contaminated:**
- "Something shifted between them."
- "There was something dangerous in his eyes."
- "She felt something she couldn't name."

**Why it fails:** It is a placeholder masquerading as mystique. The writer's job is to name the thing —
or to render it so precisely that the reader names it themselves.

**What to do instead:** Identify the specific sensation, or describe its physical manifestation.
- "Something shifted between them." → "He stopped refilling her glass. She noticed."


### 10. The "Not Quite" Hedge

**Contaminated:**
- "Not quite a smile."
- "Not quite a threat, not quite a promise."
- "Something not quite like fear."

**Why it fails:** It is imprecision dressed as subtlety. Doubles down on the Negative Parallelism
problem. Tells the reader what the thing ISN'T without committing to what it IS.

**What to do instead:** Find the precise word, or describe the physical reality.
- "Not quite a smile." → "The corner of his mouth moved — acknowledgment, not warmth."


## III. EMOTIONAL SHORTCUTS

Lazy ways of conveying internal states.

### 11. Breath-and-Body Autopilot

Defaulting to the same handful of physical responses for every emotional beat.

**The usual offenders:**
- jaw clenching / unclenching
- breath catching / releasing a breath they didn't know they were holding
- eyes darkening
- stomach tightening / dropping
- hands curling into fists
- heart hammering / pounding
- swallowing hard
- throat tightening

**Why it fails:** These are generic physiological responses. They tell the reader "emotion is happening"
without specifying WHICH emotion or making it particular to THIS character.

**What to do instead:** Tie the physical response to the character's specific psychology, history, or
circumstance. What does THIS person do when afraid? Maybe she counts ceiling tiles. Maybe he starts
humming the wrong song. The body tells the truth, but it tells it in dialect, not in generic.


### 12. The Sentimental Summary

Closing a scene or piece by spelling out its emotional meaning.

**Contaminated:**
- "In the end, what mattered wasn't the money — it was the people."
- "And for the first time in years, she felt whole."
- "He realized that home wasn't a place. It was a feeling."

**Why it fails:** It does the reader's work for them, and does it badly. The summary is always less
interesting than the scene it summarizes. It flattens ambiguity into a greeting card.

**What to do instead:** End on action, image, or dialogue. Let the scene's meaning remain in the scene.
If you've written well, the reader already knows. If you haven't, no summary will save it.


## IV. DRAMATIC FAILURES

Patterns that undermine tension, surprise, and narrative originality.

### 13. Em-Dash Addiction

Using em-dashes for false pauses, fake drama, and interruptions that aren't.

**Contaminated:**
- "She turned to him — slowly — and smiled."
- "The answer was simple — too simple."
- "He knew — had always known — that this would end badly."

**Why it fails:** One em-dash is punctuation. Three per paragraph is a nervous habit. They fragment
sentences that should flow and create "dramatic" pauses that the content doesn't support.

**What to do instead:** Use em-dashes sparingly — for genuine interruption, genuine aside, or genuine
shift. If the sentence works without the dash, remove it.


### 14. Predictable Reversals and Twists

The "surprise" ending or revelation that any experienced reader sees coming from the first paragraph.

**Common offenders:**
- The mentor was the villain all along
- The "dead" character is alive
- The protagonist realizes they were wrong and the antagonist was right
- The hard exterior hides a soft heart (revealed in a single scene)
- The twist that "it was all a dream/simulation/memory"

**Why it fails:** These are not surprises. They are the first idea. AI defaults to the most statistically
common "twist" — which means the twist the reader has seen a hundred times.

**What to do instead:** Complicate rather than reverse. Real surprise comes from characters acting
in ways that are unexpected AND inevitable — choices that the reader didn't predict but immediately
recognizes as true. The best twists don't flip the story; they deepen it.


### 15. The Safe Metaphor

Reaching for metaphors that are comfortable, expected, and therefore invisible.

**Contaminated:**
- "a tapestry of experiences"
- "the weight of his words"
- "a storm of emotions"
- "she carried the scars"
- "the light at the end of the tunnel"
- "walls around her heart"
- "drowning in grief"
- "fire in her eyes"

**Why it fails:** These metaphors have been used so often they no longer function as metaphors. The
reader's mind skips over them the way it skips over "said." They add words without adding vision.

**What to do instead:** Look at the actual thing. What does THIS grief feel like to THIS character
in THIS moment? Maybe it feels like being in a room where someone moved all the furniture two inches
to the left. Wrong, but you can't prove it. THAT is a metaphor that does work.


## V. THE STANDARD — What to Aim For

Avoiding patterns is necessary but insufficient. The goal is prose that is:

**Specific.** Every detail chosen because it is the RIGHT detail, not a PLAUSIBLE detail. Not "a flower"
but "a carnation going brown at the edges." Not "a car" but "a Civic with a cracked windshield and a
parking ticket under the wiper."

**Surprising.** Not in the sense of gimmicks or twists, but in the sense that the reader encounters
something they didn't expect — a word choice, an observation, an emotional response that is true but
not obvious.

**Earned.** Every emotional beat set up by what came before. No shortcuts. No telling the reader what
to feel. The scene does the work.

**Varied in rhythm.** Long sentences followed by short. Dense paragraphs followed by spare. The rhythm
changes because the content demands it, not because a template says "alternate."

**Brave.** Willing to sit in discomfort, ambiguity, ugliness, and silence. Willing to let a scene end
without resolution. Willing to let a character be wrong without correcting them. Willing to say nothing
when nothing is the right thing to say.


## VI. REVIEW PROTOCOL

After generating any prose, run this quick mental scan:

1. **Count your threes.** If you used tricolons, cut at least half of them.
2. **Search for "something."** Replace every instance with a specific noun or image.
3. **Check your closes.** Does any paragraph end with a one-liner meant to "land"? Does it earn the space?
4. **Audit your metaphors.** Would a thoughtful reader roll their eyes? Replace.
5. **Read for body language.** How many jaws clenched? How many breaths caught? Diversify.
6. **Kill your dashes.** If you used more than two em-dashes in a passage, cut to one or zero.
7. **Check for "not quite" and "it's not X, it's Y."** Rewrite with direct, precise language.
8. **Read the last line.** If it sounds like it belongs on a motivational poster, rewrite it.
9. **Read the whole thing aloud.** Does it sound like a person wrote it, or like a machine
   performing the idea of writing? Rewrite until it sounds human.
